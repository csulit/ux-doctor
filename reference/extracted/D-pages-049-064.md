## Sender representation in lists (continuation of previous chapter) (p.49)

### D-01 Represent senders with photos/logos, not plain text/initials  (p.49)
- **Rule**: In message/inbox lists, represent each sender with an image: a photo for people, a logo for companies. Row layout shown (from visual): avatar at left, sender name bold with date right-aligned on the same line, subject line below, one-line truncated preview (grey, smaller, ellipsis) below that; rows separated by thin, light divider lines inset from the card edges; list sits in a white rounded card with soft shadow on a very pale background.
- **Why**: Users can easily identify the sender and quickly understand the context of the message.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (thumbs-up, the only example on this page): Sarah Johnson and John Doe with round photo avatars, Starbucks and Lyft with round brand logos. (The "don't" counterpart is on the previous page, outside this range.)
- **Audit check**: Does a list of people/companies (inbox, contacts, activity feed, comments) show only text or generic/placeholder icons when a photo or logo is available?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a ~32px circular avatar (photo or logo) at the leading edge of each row; fall back to initials only when no image exists.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: imagery, cards, hierarchy, craft

## Depth and texture – overview (p.50)

### D-02 Use depth and texture (shadows, outlines, background colour) to guide eyes and actions  (p.50)
- **Rule**: Treat depth and texture as core design principles. Create them through shadows, outlines or varying background colours so they guide where users look and what they do.
- **Why**: Beyond aesthetics they improve usability: they build visual hierarchy and make interactive elements more noticeable.
- **Do / Don't**: Do use depth cues to separate layers/sections and to flag interactive elements; don't leave an interface as one undifferentiated flat plane.
- **Audit check**: Can you tell, without reading, which elements are raised/interactive versus background? If every surface is the same colour with no shadow, outline or tone change, flag it.
- **Restyle fix**: Introduce a layering model: page background tone, surface (cards) tone, optional outline, and shadow tiers for floating elements (see D-08/D-09).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: depth, hierarchy, shadows, borders, color

## Use shadows to add depth (p.50-51)

### D-03 Give floating elements (dropdown menus) a subtle shadow  (p.50-51)
- **Rule**: Use shadows to create a sense of depth so users can tell which items they can click or move, and to move the design away from flat layouts. A navigation dropdown should have a subtle shadow so it appears to float above the page.
- **Why**: The shadow makes the dropdown seem to float above the page and helps the menu stand out from the background, making it easier to focus on the options.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (left, thumbs-down): nav bar "Resources / Solutions / Pricing" with the open "Solutions" dropdown (Marketing, Product & Engineering, Customer experience, Customer engagement, footer "All resources") has no shadow, so the white panel merges with the pale grey background behind it. Do (right, thumbs-up): identical dropdown with a subtle, diffuse shadow that lifts it off the page.
- **Audit check**: Do dropdowns, popovers, menus, tooltips and modals have a box-shadow (or equivalent elevation) when overlapping other content? `box-shadow: none` on an absolutely/fixed-positioned overlay = violation.
- **Restyle fix**: Add a soft shadow to the overlay (see D-05 values, e.g. `0 12px 48px` low-opacity) so it separates from the underlying page.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: depth, shadows, navigation, interaction-cost

### D-04 Add shadows to elements overlapping dynamic/complex backgrounds  (p.51)
- **Rule**: When an element sits on top of a complex or variable background (e.g. a map), give it a shadow so it does not get lost. Example: the "Active users – Past week – 128,200" label over the US on a map, and the zoom +/- buttons at the bottom of the map.
- **Why**: Without shadows these elements risk blending into the complex, variable background.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (left): map card where the 128,200 label chip and the +/- zoom buttons are flat white on the map, nearly blending in. Do (right): the same label chip and zoom buttons carry a soft shadow and visibly sit above the map.
- **Audit check**: Are labels, chips, controls or text containers placed over images/maps/video/charts without any shadow, border, or opaque contrast background?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a small soft shadow (sm tier, see D-09) to overlay chips and map controls.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: depth, shadows, imagery, contrast

## Shadow quality and colour (p.52-54)

### D-05 Use soft, diffuse shadows – never harsh/tight dark ones  (p.52)
- **Rule**: Shadows must be soft and blend with the background, adding just enough depth. Harsh or too-distinct shadows look overbearing and unprofessional. Reference values (Figma drop shadow, white card on very light grey page):
  - Bad: X 0, Y 0, Blur 48, colour #9F9F9F (strong, rough, dark grey halo all around the card).
  - Good: X 0, Y 12, Blur 48, colour #E9E9E9 (lighter, smoother, offset downward).
- **Why**: A common mistake is shadows too harsh/distinct; they create an overbearing effect and look unprofessional.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't: product card ("Only natural ingredients", lemon + dropper bottle image, yellow "Learn more" button) with a dark, wide, centred (Y 0) grey shadow. Do: same card with a very light grey shadow shifted down 12px (light comes from above) and the same 48 blur.
- **Audit check**: Is the shadow colour darker than ~#D0D0D0 equivalent (or alpha above roughly 10-15% black) (inference) on a light UI? Is Y offset 0 with large blur (halo all around)? Large blur (~48px) with light colour and positive Y offset (~12px) is the target pattern.
- **Restyle fix**: Replace with `box-shadow: 0 12px 48px <very light tint>` (e.g. #E9E9E9 on neutral bg), or lower alpha of existing shadow and add a positive Y offset.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: shadows, depth, craft

### D-06 Tint the shadow colour to match a coloured background  (p.53-54)
- **Rule**: Match shadow colour to the background hue instead of using neutral grey. Example on a light purple background: grey shadow (X 0, Y 19, Blur 48, #C7C7C7) vs purple-tinted shadow (X 0, Y 19, Blur 56, #CFC9DD).
- **Why**: Colour matters as much as softness/depth; grey looks like a safe bet but clashes with the purple background, breaking visual harmony and feeling jarring. A tinted shadow blends seamlessly and makes the design more cohesive.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't: "Be You, Only Better" card (woman with purple-lit image, yellow "Get flawless today" button) on pale purple page with a grey #C7C7C7 shadow. Do: same card with a purple-tinted #CFC9DD shadow and slightly larger blur (56 vs 48). Described as a "pro tip many junior designers don't know".
- **Audit check**: On any non-neutral page/section background, is the shadow colour a neutral grey/black (R=G=B) rather than a darker, low-saturation version of the background hue? Compare shadow hue with background hue.
- **Restyle fix**: Set shadow colour to a desaturated mid-tone of the background hue (e.g. #CFC9DD for a light purple surface) and keep it soft; optionally widen blur a few px.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: shadows, color, depth, craft

### D-07 Never use pure grey or black shadows on coloured backgrounds (yellow example)  (p.54)
- **Rule**: Once you know the tinting trick, don't use pure grey/black shadows on coloured backgrounds. Second worked example on a pale yellow/cream background: grey shadow (X 0, Y 20, Blur 60, #D8D8D8) vs warm-tinted shadow (X 0, Y 12, Blur 48, #E3D5B2).
- **Why**: Creates more coherent and engaging UIs; an easy but impactful improvement.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't: "Only natural ingredients" card (amber bottles-and-arch image, yellow "Learn more" button) on a cream background with neutral grey shadow, which looks dirty against the warm tone. Do: same card with a warm beige shadow (#E3D5B2) that sits naturally in the cream background.
- **Audit check**: Any shadow with equal R/G/B channels (grey/black, e.g. rgba(0,0,0,x)) over a tinted section background.
- **Restyle fix**: Swap neutral shadow for a warm/hue-matched tint (e.g. #E3D5B2 on cream) at similar softness; note the tinted version also used smaller Y (12 vs 20) and blur (48 vs 60).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: shadows, color, depth

## Shadow system (p.55-56)

### D-08 Define a shadow system (named elevation scale) and use it consistently  (p.55)
- **Rule**: Create a shadow system: a rulebook of shadow styles applied the same way every time across all projects, matching your brand style (simple, flashy, or in between). Example scale shown has six named levels: xs, sm, md, lg, xl, 2xl.
- **Why**: Keeps the design consistent and in line with the brand, and improves user experience.
- **Do / Don't**: Visual shows six identical white squares on a pale grey panel with progressively larger/softer/lower shadows: xs = tight, crisp, almost outline-like; sm and md = small soft shadow beneath; lg = medium diffused; xl and 2xl = large, wide, strongly offset diffuse shadows (from visual; exact values not given).
- **Audit check**: Count distinct `box-shadow` values in the CSS/tokens. Ad-hoc, one-off values per component (many near-duplicates) = violation; expect a small defined set (e.g. xs/sm/md/lg/xl/2xl or at least 3 tiers (inference)) exposed as tokens.
- **Restyle fix**: Consolidate shadows into named tokens (e.g. `--shadow-xs … --shadow-2xl` / Tailwind `shadow-*`) with monotonic increase in Y offset and blur, then map each component to a token.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: shadows, depth, craft, cards

### D-09 Have at least three shadow tiers mapped to element purpose  (p.56)
- **Rule**: Start with at least three styles:
  1. Soft shadow – general elements you want to slightly lift off the page: buttons, small cards, thumbnails.
  2. Medium shadow – elements that need more focus: modals, pop-up windows. A bit darker and with more spread than soft.
  3. Strong shadow – elements that must really stand out / appear elevated: dropdown menus, important alerts.
- **Why**: Elevation should reflect importance/focus level of the element.
- **Do / Don't**: Do map tier to element role (small & static = soft; focus-demanding overlays = medium; highest, most prominent = strong). Don't use the same shadow for everything, or a heavier shadow on minor elements than on overlays.
- **Audit check**: Is a modal's shadow weaker than a small card's? Do buttons/thumbnails carry large, dark shadows? Is there a visible difference between button, modal and dropdown elevations?
- **Restyle fix**: Assign soft to buttons/small cards/thumbnails, medium to modals/popups, strong to dropdowns/important alerts; increase blur/spread/darkness with tier.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: shadows, depth, hierarchy

### D-10 Create variations of shadow styles for specific needs  (p.56)
- **Rule**: Each shadow tier can have variants for specific needs, e.g. a medium shadow with a different colour tint for warning messages, or a really subtle shadow for text boxes.
- **Why**: Shadows should be adaptable to context while staying within the system.
- **Do / Don't**: Do derive variants (tinted, extra-subtle) from the base tiers; don't invent unrelated one-off shadows.
- **Audit check**: Do warning/alert components and inputs use a variant derived from the system (tinted or subtler), or an unrelated hard-coded shadow?
- **Restyle fix**: Add token variants such as `shadow-md-warning` (tinted) and `shadow-input` (very subtle).
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: shadows, color, forms, states

## Depth through outlines and background colours (p.56-58)

### D-11 Use background-colour layering (light grey page + white cards) instead of all-white  (p.56-57)
- **Rule**: Create depth by subtly changing background colour to distinguish sections/elements. Pure white for page and elements can work but can look too plain. Use a light grey page background with white cards on top for a layered effect. Especially valuable in information-dense interfaces such as dashboards, but not limited to them.
- **Why**: Gives a more structured look and makes it easier for users to focus on specific sections.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (top, thumbs-down): "Tasking" dashboard where main content area, sidebar, KPI stats (Sales $92,200 / Website visits 121,520), and Customer Growth chart are all on one flat white surface with no separation. Do (bottom, thumbs-up): content area has a light grey background, with Sales, Website visits and Customer Growth each in separate white cards; sidebar is white and divided from content by a thin vertical line (from visual).
- **Audit check**: Is the page background identical to card/panel surface colour (e.g. #FFFFFF on #FFFFFF) with no border or shadow separating regions? In dashboards, are widgets/sections floating on one white plane?
- **Restyle fix**: Set page/canvas background to a very light grey (e.g. ~#F5F5F7-ish, from visual; no value given) and keep cards/panels pure white.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: depth, color, layout, cards, hierarchy

### D-12 Alternatively segment areas with thin outlines  (p.58)
- **Rule**: Outlines can create the same separation as background colour: use thin lines to segment areas, guide the eye and create natural divisions. Applied example (from visual): each widget (Sales, Website visits, Customer Growth) is in a card with a thin light outline; sidebar sections are divided by thin horizontal hairlines; a thin vertical line divides sidebar from content; the filter dropdown ("Current year") has a thin outline.
- **Why**: Helps navigate complex interfaces more easily and adds a touch of sophistication. Both techniques (colour and outline) enhance usability as well as aesthetics.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (thumbs-up): dashboard on white with thin, light outlines around cards and dividers between sidebar groups. Don't: leave sections unseparated (see D-11 bad example).
- **Audit check**: Are there flat white regions of different purpose with no separation by tone, outline or shadow? Are outline widths 1px (inference) and colour low-contrast?
- **Restyle fix**: Add `border: 1px solid <light grey>` to cards and `border-top/bottom` hairlines between sidebar groups, keeping the outline light (see D-14).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: borders, depth, layout, cards

## Borders (p.59-62)

### D-13 Don't overdo borders and background colours  (p.59-60)
- **Rule**: Don't overuse borders or multiple background colours per section. Rely on colour and generous white space to separate sections (e.g. results from buttons).
- **Why**: Overuse of borders clutters the interface and makes it busy, confusing or outdated; excess lines and colours detract from the content, make the UI overcomplicated and harder to navigate, and overwhelm the user. Smart use of white space and background colours achieves the same separation and focus without visual noise. Borders are fine when used sparingly and intentionally – find the balance.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (left, thumbs-down): "Pick your flavours" modal with a divider under the title, a bordered pill search field between two full-width lines, a divider line between every flavour row (Raspberry $2.40, Apricot $2.10, Orange $1.80, Grape $1.30, Papaya $4.20), a grey-tinted footer region with a top line holding Cancel/Continue. Do (right, thumbs-up): same modal with no borders: search field uses a light grey fill, rows separated only by spacing, footer on white with generous whitespace; Continue is a filled purple pill, Cancel plain text.
- **Audit check**: Count borders/dividers per component; flag a divider between every list row plus section borders plus differently coloured section backgrounds in the same card. Flag a bordered input on top of a bordered/dividered header. Could the same separation be achieved with spacing or a subtle fill?
- **Restyle fix**: Remove row dividers and section borders; replace with spacing (increase vertical padding), use a light grey fill for the search input, drop footer background tint.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: borders, spacing, craft, hierarchy, forms

### D-14 Keep borders thin and light  (p.60-61)
- **Rule**: Borders should be thin and light; subtlety is key. Avoid thick, dark or prominent borders.
- **Why**: Thick, dark, prominent borders overpower content and make the interface feel heavy and outdated. Thin, light borders define sections without stealing the spotlight, keep the aesthetic light and open, are easier on the eyes and give a more pleasant experience. "Less is often more."
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (thumbs-down): "Recent orders" table (columns Customer ID, Customer name, Item name, Order date, Status [Paid/Pending/Cancel pills], Price; Previous / pagination 1 2 3 … 8 9 10 / Next) with dark, full-strength row lines and dark-outlined Filter/Previous/Next buttons. Do (thumbs-up): same table with pale hairline row separators and light-grey outlines on Filter/Previous/Next buttons; the data stays the focus.
- **Audit check**: Border colour darker than a light grey (e.g. near-black/dark grey `#000`/`#333` borders on table rows or buttons) or width above 1px on routine dividers (no exact threshold in PDF; examples use 1px light lines).
- **Restyle fix**: Set borders to `1px solid` very light grey (low contrast with background, e.g. around #E5E7EB-level, from visual) for table rows and secondary buttons.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: borders, tables, buttons, craft, contrast

### D-15 It is OK to break the border rules deliberately (e.g. bold 10px soft border)  (p.62)
- **Rule**: You may break the "thin and light" rule to achieve a specific style/brand image, provided the result stays balanced and the line stays smooth and soft. Example: flight search box with either a subtle 1px outline border or a bold 10px border (a soft, pale-blue translucent frame) – both are valid.
- **Why**: Both designs achieve different effects for different styles, yet both work because they maintain balance; even a thick border doesn't overpower when it is smooth and soft. It's all about balance and alignment with overall design goals; break rules thoughtfully and deliberately.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (both thumbs-up): (1) flight search box (Return/One way, Economy class, 1 passenger, 2 luggages; From Vienna VIE → To London STN; Departure Friday 17 June → Return Sunday 19 June; blue Search button) with a 1px outline and soft shadow; (2) same box inside a 10px soft, pale blue/pinkish blurred frame. Don't: apply a thick border that is dark/hard and overpowering (implied).
- **Audit check**: If a border is thick (>2px (inference)), is it soft/low-contrast and consistent with the brand style, or is it dark and harsh? Is the exception intentional and consistent across the product?
- **Restyle fix**: If a thick border is wanted, make it a translucent, low-contrast tint of the brand colour with soft edges; otherwise revert to 1px light.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: borders, craft, color

## Glass effect (p.63-64)

### D-16 Use glassmorphism only when it fits the brand and benefits users  (p.63)
- **Rule**: The glass ("glassmorphism") effect – UI elements that look like frosted/blurred glass – can add a modern, futuristic touch, depth and texture, realism and sophistication that flat designs lack. But it's not one-size-fits-all: decide whether it aligns with the brand and benefits users.
- **Why**: Can elevate the design and make it more dynamic and engaging, but may be inappropriate for some brands.
- **Do / Don't**: Do consider it for e.g. a SaaS company (modern, fresh). Don't force it on e.g. a luxury cosmetics brand that may prefer a traditional or minimalistic approach.
- **Audit check**: Is a glass/blur style present that conflicts with brand tone, or applied everywhere without purpose?
- **Restyle fix**: Apply backdrop blur/translucent fill only to selected floating components over rich imagery; keep brand-conservative products flat/minimal.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: depth, imagery, craft

### D-17 A flat, plain background is a valid baseline (media player on dark grey)  (p.63)
- **Rule**: A component on a flat, plain background is clean, readable and does its job well. Example: "Life of parrots" player (Documentary · 1 hr 3 min, 02:15 / 04:32, green progress bar, pause, volume, fullscreen icons, photo thumbnail of a scarlet macaw) as a near-black card on a flat dark grey page.
- **Why**: Clean, readable, does its job – glass is an enhancement, not a requirement.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (thumbs-up): opaque dark player on flat dark-grey background; high legibility of white title and grey text.
- **Audit check**: Where glass effect is absent and the background is plain, don't flag it as a defect; only recommend glass if it adds purpose.
- **Restyle fix**: Keep as is, or upgrade to glass (D-18) only when there is a rich image behind the component.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: depth, contrast, craft

### D-18 When using glass, keep text legible and the effect balanced – modern but not overbearing  (p.64)
- **Rule**: The glass effect should serve a purpose: elevate the visual appeal of the component so it stands out without compromising user experience; be modern but not overbearing, eye-catching but not distracting. Good example: the same player as a translucent, blurred, darkly tinted green glass panel with subtle light border over a dark, deep-green jungle photo – white title, preview text and controls remain readable.
- **Why**: Adds sophistication and high-tech allure while keeping usability.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (thumbs-up): glass panel over a dark jungle photo; text clearly legible. Don't (thumbs-down, no text explanation – from visual): the same glass panel over a bright, light teal/misty jungle photo; the panel is pale, white text and secondary text/progress bar lose contrast (the "Documentary · 1 hr 3 min" line, timestamps and controls become faint), so the effect becomes hard to read.
- **Audit check**: Measure text/control contrast on top of translucent/backdrop-blur surfaces over the brightest region of the background (WCAG AA 4.5:1 for text is the standard check; the PDF gives no number). Is the tint/blur strong enough? Does secondary text vanish on light imagery?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a darker tint (higher fill opacity) and/or stronger backdrop blur to the glass panel, or darken/overlay the background image; keep a faint 1px light border for edge definition.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: depth, contrast, imagery, accessibility
