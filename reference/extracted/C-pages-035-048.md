## Forms: mirror the final output (p.35)

### C-01 Lay out creation forms to mirror the final result  (p.35)
- **Rule**: When users create content that will be displayed later (e.g. a marketplace listing), do not default to a standard linear stack of labelled fields. Arrange the form fields the way the information will appear on the final page (e.g. image area on top, item name and price side by side, then description, location, tag chip).
- **Why**: Gives users a sense of how the listing will look and function, making form-filling more relatable and engaging.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (thumbs-down): a plain white card titled "Create new listing" with a vertical list of label + empty input pairs (Item, Price, Description, Categories, Location, Images with a "Browse" button). Do (thumbs-up, from visual): a card that looks like the finished listing, with a large grey image placeholder ("Add an image") at top, "Item" ("What are you selling?") and "Price" ("$0") in one row separated by a divider, then "Description" ("Describe what you're selling in more detail ..."), "Location" ("Enter your address"), and an "Add tag +" pill at the bottom. Fields are inline-placeholder style, separated by thin dividers.
- **Audit check**: Is a "create/list/post" form a generic linear label-over-input stack even though a known output layout (listing, profile, card, post) exists? Does field order/grouping differ from the order in which the data is shown on the result page?
- **Restyle fix**: Rebuild the form as an editable preview of the output: image slot at the top, title/price in one row, description below, location, tags; use placeholder text as labels where appropriate and thin dividers instead of boxed inputs.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: forms, layout, interaction-cost

## Balance and harmony (p.36-37)

### C-02 Keep proportion and scale balanced – no element overpowers the others  (p.36)
- **Rule**: No single element should overpower the others. Every element gets its own space and prominence, but not so much that it becomes a distraction.
- **Why**: Balance and harmony make everything on the page fit together, make the design look good, easier to use, and help people quickly understand what is on screen and how to use it.
- **Do / Don't**: See C-04/C-05 (kiwi card example, p.37). Oversized elements are the "don't".
- **Audit check**: Is any one element (hero image, CTA, heading) disproportionately large relative to the rest of the component so that the eye has no clear second stop? Compare sizes of image, heading, body and button within a card.
- **Restyle fix**: Scale down the dominant element until the heading, body and CTA each remain easy to see without any of them dominating.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, layout, craft

### C-03 Use colour harmony – don't overuse a single colour  (p.36)
- **Rule**: Overusing one colour makes a design feel monotonous or overwhelming. Use complementary or analogous colours to bring variation and emotional balance.
- **Why**: Variation creates emotional balance; a single colour everywhere is monotonous/overwhelming.
- **Do / Don't**: See C-06 (kiwi example).
- **Audit check**: Is one hue used for image background, CTA and small accents all at once? Is the palette limited to a single hue with no complementary/analogous partner?
- **Restyle fix**: Reserve the strong accent for the primary action; use a softer/neutral or analogous/complementary tint for backgrounds and decorative elements.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: color, hierarchy

### C-04 Keep consistent spacing, typography and element sizes between components  (p.36)
- **Rule**: Maintain consistent spacing, typography and element sizes to create a harmonious relationship among all UI components ("element relationships").
- **Why**: Consistent relationships make the components feel like one system.
- **Do / Don't**: No dedicated example; the kiwi pair (p.37) shows the refined version with heading, body, rating and button at modest, proportionate sizes.
- **Audit check**: Do similar components use different paddings, font sizes or element heights? Are spacing/type/size values drawn from a shared scale/tokens?
- **Restyle fix**: Normalise spacing, type sizes and element sizes to a shared token scale across components.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: spacing, typography, craft

### C-05 Don't let the hero image and CTA button hog the card  (p.37)
- **Rule**: Image and button must not be too big compared to the rest of the content. Keep the image eye-catching but not overshadowing; make heading and CTA easy to see but "not too flashy".
- **Why**: When image and button are oversized, they hog attention; the eye doesn't know where to go next and crucial details (heading, description, rating) get lost.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual): kiwi-ice-cream card with a very large circle image, tiny description and rating text, and a full-card-width tall pill "Get now" button. Do (from visual): the image is smaller, the heading stays about the same size, the description and rating text are larger and more readable (the description grows relative to the heading), the rating row sits under it and the "Get now" button is a small, compact pill (not full width), the card is shorter.
- **Audit check**: Is the CTA full-width/very tall on a content card when it is not a mobile footer bar? Is the hero image taking the dominant share of card area while the description and rating text are tiny (compare description/rating size to the heading and the image)? Does the secondary content get visually lost?
- **Restyle fix**: Reduce image diameter/height, shrink CTA to content-width pill with normal padding, bump the description and rating text size (the heading can stay about the same) so the card reads heading → description → rating → CTA.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, buttons, imagery, layout

### C-06 Don't paint everything the same colour (single-colour overuse)  (p.37)
- **Rule**: Don't use one colour (e.g. green) for the image background, the CTA and smaller elements simultaneously; distribute colours more evenly.
- **Why**: Overuse of a single colour makes the interface feel monotonous and a bit overwhelming.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual): saturated green circle behind the image, green decorative bits and a big green CTA. Do (from visual): image sits on a pale peach/cream circle with small pink/green confetti shapes; only the "Get now" button carries the green (lime) accent. Colours more even, not leaning on one hue.
- **Audit check**: Count surfaces/elements sharing the same saturated accent hue within one component; flag if the accent is on both a large background shape and the CTA (and other small items).
- **Restyle fix**: Replace large saturated backgrounds with a light neutral/complementary tint; keep the saturated accent for the CTA only.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: color, hierarchy

## Consistency (p.38-46)

### C-07 Keep essential UI components consistent across the same and different screens  (p.38)
- **Rule**: Make elements function and look similar throughout the design – on the same screen and across screens. Consistency does not mean everything is identical (e.g. product pictures may have different backgrounds), but essential components – titles, buttons, input fields – must remain consistent.
- **Why**: (1) Ease of use – easier navigation; (2) Builds trust – looks more professional and reliable; (3) Reduces cognitive load – less mental effort; (4) Cohesive aesthetic – unified experience that resonates with users. Users learn the UI faster, improving usability and satisfaction.
- **Do / Don't**: Do vary content imagery (backgrounds of product pictures); don't vary the look/behaviour of titles, buttons, inputs.
- **Audit check**: Do the same component types (title, button, input) have different size, radius, colour, padding or behaviour on different screens/sections? Are there one-off component variants not in the design system?
- **Restyle fix**: Consolidate to a single component style per role (title, button, input) via shared tokens/components; allow variation only in content/imagery.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: craft, interaction-cost, hierarchy

### C-08 Use one corner-radius/shape language for all elements  (p.39)
- **Rule**: Use consistent corner radii across pictures, buttons and other elements; don't mix e.g. rounded pictures with square buttons.
- **Why**: Inconsistent radii confuse users, make the design look off-brand and fail to create a cohesive look. A consistent rounded appearance makes the UI easier to navigate and more pleasing (p.40 continuation).
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual, three animal cards): lion picture is a circle but its "Book now" button is a square-cornered black rectangle; the hippo picture is a square image; the third card's avatars are square-ish and the middle card edge is square-cornered; others are rounded. Do (from visual): all three cards have circular images, pill-shaped black "Book now" buttons, rounded card corners and rounded avatars.
- **Audit check**: Collect all `border-radius` values (rounded-*, radius tokens) in a view; flag if sibling cards/buttons/images mix sharp (0) with rounded values or have more radii than the token scale (e.g. a button radius 0 next to rounded-full buttons).
- **Restyle fix**: Define radius tokens (e.g. card radius, button radius, media radius) and apply the same shape family (all rounded or all sharp) to cards, buttons, images, avatars.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: borders, craft, buttons, cards

### C-09 Use one button colour scheme (primary/secondary/tertiary) everywhere  (p.40-41)
- **Rule**: Don't use many different colours for primary, secondary and tertiary buttons (e.g. tinted to match each card's image). Use one colour scheme for all buttons: black (dark) for main actions and light grey for secondary ones.
- **Why**: Different button colours make users wonder whether each button has a unique function, divert attention from main content and can feel off-brand. One scheme looks organised/professional and makes it obvious what each button does.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual): each of the three cards has its own button pair coloured to match the illustration (orange for lion, brown for hippo, purple for elephant; light tint "Learn more" + solid "Book now"). Do (from visual): all three cards use the same pair – light grey "Learn more" and near-black/dark brown "Book now".
- **Audit check**: Do equivalent buttons (same role) have different background colours across sibling cards/sections? Count distinct primary-button fills per screen (should be 1).
- **Restyle fix**: Define one primary and one secondary button style (dark fill / light-grey fill) and apply to every card regardless of illustration colour.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: buttons, color, craft, hierarchy

### C-10 Normalise image shapes and sizes by placing them in a consistent container (e.g. circle)  (p.41-42)
- **Rule**: When images vary in shape and size, put them inside a consistent shape such as a circle, then adjust their size/positioning so they are the same size and in similar positions.
- **Why**: Varied shapes/sizes look out of place and disconnected with no unity or flow; the circle gives a cleaner, more put-together look.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual): three gift-box illustrations of different widths/heights floating on white, at slightly different vertical positions. Do (from visual): each gift box sits in a same-size circle with a soft tint (pale blue, pale pink, pale yellow), centred at the same spot above the title.
- **Audit check**: Do images/illustrations in a row of cards have different dimensions, aspect ratios or alignment? Is there a shared fixed-size mask/container (same width/height/radius)?
- **Restyle fix**: Wrap each image in a fixed-size circular (or other uniform) container with a subtle tint and centre the image; keep the same offset from the card top.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: imagery, cards, alignment, craft

### C-11 Make cards in a set (3+) the same height  (p.43)
- **Rule**: In a layout with three or more cards, all cards must have the same height.
- **Why**: Looks good and makes things easier and more straightforward for the user.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual): three accommodation cards (Forest Bungalows, Trailer Homes, Mountain Lodges in the Snowy Region of Scotland) of three different heights because the text lengths differ (short, shortest, longest) – ragged bottom edges and buttons at different y positions. Do: see C-12/C-13 (equal-height cards).
- **Audit check**: In a card grid/row of 3+, compare rendered heights and CTA y-offsets; flag if not equal (code: missing `align-items: stretch`/`h-full`/grid equal rows).
- **Restyle fix**: Use grid/flex stretch so cards share the tallest card's height (or fixed heights) and see C-13 for bottom-aligned CTAs.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: cards, layout, alignment

### C-12 Keep headings and descriptions about the same length across cards  (p.43-44)
- **Rule**: Make headings and descriptions about the same length so cards naturally share the same height; if you can influence UX copy, keep the same number of lines across all cards.
- **Why**: Keeps cards the same height and makes the design look more balanced.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (p.43): card 3 title wraps to two lines ("Mountain Lodges in the Snowy Region of Scotland") with a long ~7-line paragraph, while the other titles are 1 line and their descriptions are 4 lines (Forest Bungalows) and 2 lines (Trailer Homes). Do (from visual p.44): titles shortened to "Forest Bungalows", "Trailer Homes", "Mountain Lodges"; each description is ~2 lines ("Surround yourself with the calming vibes of the forest in our cozy bungalows." / "Enjoy a nice stay in our comfy trailer homes. All the essentials without the big price tag." / "Stay in luxury among stunning mountains, where rustic charm meets modern comfort."); all cards equal height with buttons aligned.
- **Audit check**: Compare line counts of titles/descriptions among sibling cards; flag if they differ (e.g. more than a line difference (inference)) or text is much longer in one card.
- **Restyle fix**: Edit/trim copy so each title and description has the same line count (shorten the outlier); otherwise use C-13.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: copy, cards, layout

### C-13 When copy length varies, equalise card height and pin CTAs to the bottom  (p.44)
- **Rule**: If you can't control UX copy and content varies, make all cards the same height but align the CTA buttons at the bottom of each card, leaving any extra space in the middle (between text and button).
- **Why**: Creates the illusion of uniformity even when content length varies.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (from visual p.44 bottom): three cards with different text lengths (Mountain Lodges long, Forest Bungalows medium, Trailer Homes medium), equal height, "Explore offers" buttons all on the same baseline at the bottom with empty space above the button in cards with shorter text. Don't: buttons directly under variable-length text at different heights (p.43 bad example).
- **Audit check**: In equal-height cards, are CTAs at identical y positions? (Code: card is flex column and CTA wrapper has `margin-top:auto`/`mt-auto`.) Flag CTAs that float at different heights.
- **Restyle fix**: Make the card `display:flex; flex-direction:column; height:100%` and push the button container with `margin-top:auto`.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: cards, alignment, buttons, layout

### C-14 Use a single icon style (outline vs filled, weight, detail, size) within a screen  (p.45)
- **Rule**: Don't mix icons of different sizes or types – e.g. one simple and another highly detailed, or some outlined and others filled on the same screen. Decide on an icon style ahead of time and use it across all UI screens (e.g. outlined everywhere).
- **Why**: Inconsistent icons are a common error that breaks cohesion.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual, "Account features" list): Manage account icon is a detailed double-person outline, search is a different outline, heart is filled, share is filled, notification bell is filled – mixed outline/filled and different detail levels, and icons slightly different sizes. Do (from visual): all five icons (user, search, heart, share, bell) are consistent thin outline icons of the same size and stroke, in the same purple colour.
- **Audit check**: Within a screen, do icons mix stroke and fill variants, different stroke widths, different sizes or different libraries? (Code: multiple icon packages imported, mix of `fill`/`stroke` styles, inconsistent width/height.)
- **Restyle fix**: Replace all icons with one set in a single style (e.g. outlined), same stroke width and the same size (grid).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: icons, craft

### C-15 Mix icon styles only to signal state (e.g. filled = selected tab)  (p.45-46)
- **Rule**: Exception: mixing icon styles is OK when it indicates different states. In bottom navigation, the selected tab uses a filled icon while the other tabs use outlined icons.
- **Why**: This subtle style change serves a functional purpose – helping users quickly identify their current location in the app.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (from visual p.46): dark bottom nav bar with four icons; the active "home" icon is filled, on a lilac circular highlight raised/notched in the bar; the other three (clock, pie chart, user) are outlined and grey; annotated "Icon: filled" and "Icon: outlined".
- **Audit check**: If a nav bar uses filled vs outline icons, does the difference map to selected/unselected state rather than arbitrary variation? Is the active tab differentiated (filled)?
- **Restyle fix**: In tab bars/nav, use outlined for inactive and the filled variant of the same icon for the active tab (optionally plus highlight).
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: icons, navigation, states

### C-16 Use one icon set (custom or open-source) that fits the brand  (p.46)
- **Rule**: A custom icon set is one way to achieve consistency, but open-source icons are fine if the set aligns with the overall brand image and you don't mix two different sets with varying properties.
- **Why**: Keeps a consistent look.
- **Do / Don't**: Do: one coherent set (custom or open-source) matching the brand. Don't: mix two different icon sets with different properties (weight, style, corner shape).
- **Audit check**: How many icon libraries are in the project/Figma file? Do icons across screens have different stroke weights, terminal shapes or corner styles?
- **Restyle fix**: Pick one library (e.g. a single open-source family matching brand tone) and swap out all others.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: icons, craft

## Visual cues (p.47-48)

### C-17 Add illustrations/progress indicators to make step content faster to grasp  (p.47)
- **Rule**: Use visual cues (illustrations, step indicators) rather than text-only presentation; they do more than add flair – they help users understand the message faster, make information easier to grasp, and make the interaction more engaging and memorable.
- **Why**: Visuals make learning and interaction more engaging and memorable and improve understanding speed.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual): three "Step 1/2/3" cards ("Place Your Order", "On the Way", "Get It Delivered") with only small grey "Step N" label, title and description text on white. Do (from visual): the same cards each with a themed illustration at top (person with phone/boxes, courier on bicycle, courier handing pizza at a door) and a 3-segment progress bar under it (green segment marks the current step: 1st, 2nd or 3rd of the three) above "Step N", title and description.
- **Audit check**: Are step/process/feature sections text-only with no iconography, illustration or progress indicator? Is there a visual representation of what each step means and where the user is in the sequence?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a relevant illustration/icon per step and a segmented progress indicator highlighting the step's position.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: imagery, hierarchy, states, craft

### C-18 Represent senders with coloured initial avatars in lists (inbox/messaging)  (p.48)
- **Rule**: In email inbox/messaging lists, add a visual cue per sender: a coloured circular icon (from a set of colours) with the sender's first initial in the middle.
- **Why**: A plain text list, while functional, misses opportunities to enhance usability; avatars help scanning and distinguishing senders and make the UI creative and visually appealing.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual): list of rows (name, date right-aligned, bold subject, grey preview truncated with ellipsis) with no leading visual. Do (from visual): same rows with a leading circle containing a white initial letter – S (pink, Sarah Johnson), S (green, Starbucks), J (purple, John Doe), L (orange, Lyft); text is shifted right of the avatar, dates remain right-aligned.
- **Audit check**: Do list items (people/senders/contacts) lack avatars or leading visual identifiers? Are avatar colours distinguishable among different senders?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a circular avatar (~same height as two lines of text, from visual) with the first-letter initial in white on a per-sender colour; keep the text column aligned.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: imagery, hierarchy, craft
