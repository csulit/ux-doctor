Sources: The UI/UX Playbook (uxpeak; IDs A-… to H-…, page numbers are PDF pages) and, for W-… IDs, paraphrased tips from public UX pattern pages (cited as web:<slug>, no page). Generated by scripts/build-knowledge.mjs — do not edit by hand.

## Forms: mirror the final output (p.35)

### C-01 Lay out creation forms to mirror the final result  (p.35)
- **Rule**: When users create content that will be displayed later (e.g. a marketplace listing), do not default to a standard linear stack of labelled fields. Arrange the form fields the way the information will appear on the final page (e.g. image area on top, item name and price side by side, then description, location, tag chip).
- **Why**: Gives users a sense of how the listing will look and function, making form-filling more relatable and engaging.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (thumbs-down): a plain white card titled "Create new listing" with a vertical list of label + empty input pairs (Item, Price, Description, Categories, Location, Images with a "Browse" button). Do (thumbs-up, from visual): a card that looks like the finished listing, with a large grey image placeholder ("Add an image") at top, "Item" ("What are you selling?") and "Price" ("$0") in one row separated by a divider, then "Description" ("Describe what you're selling in more detail ..."), "Location" ("Enter your address"), and an "Add tag +" pill at the bottom. Fields are inline-placeholder style, separated by thin dividers.
- **Audit check**: Is a "create/list/post" form a generic linear label-over-input stack even though a known output layout (listing, profile, card, post) exists? Does field order/grouping differ from the order in which the data is shown on the result page?
- **Restyle fix**: Rebuild the form as an editable preview of the output: image slot at the top, title/price in one row, description below, location, tags; use placeholder text as labels where appropriate and thin dividers instead of boxed inputs.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: forms, layout, interaction-cost

## Expose content directly (p.111-113)

### G-02 Don't hide content behind promotional banners that need a tap  (p.111, p.112)
- **Rule**: Don't put a big banner like "Discover 100+ recipes selected by our chefs" or "Discover 40+ foods that are good for your health" with an "Explore now" button between the user and the actual content. Show the content itself on the screen.
- **Why**: The banner is an extra step. Interaction cost is the cognitive, physical and time effort a user spends to reach their goal. Users want immediate value, and hiding content creates an unnecessary barrier between them and the value of the app. The cost looks trivial but adds up, especially for new users who are unsure about investing time.
- **Do / Don't**:
  - Don't (p.111): the "Discover" screen has two section headings, each followed by one large full-width photo banner (pasta on red/cream; grapefruit on blue/yellow). Each banner has a copy line and a dark "Explore now" button. No real recipe or food items are visible.
  - Do (p.112): the same "Discover" screen with a "Recommended recipes" row and a "View all" link at the right. The row shows three image cards (rounded corners) with the title below and a clock icon plus time ("Noodle dish 20 minutes", "Grilled chicken with beans 20 minutes", "Baked duck and rice 32 minutes"). Then a "Recommended foods" row with "View all" and three items on light-grey rounded tiles (Tomatoes, Pistachio, Broccoli) with centred labels.
- **Audit check**: Does a home or discovery screen use large banner/hero tiles whose only job is to say "explore X" and link elsewhere? Does each section show real items (cards/thumbnails with titles and metadata) at the point of entry? Count the taps from the screen to the first real content item (should be 0 (inference)).
- **Restyle fix**: Replace the banner with a horizontal row or grid of 3 actual item cards (image, title, key metadata such as duration). Add a small "View all" link on the right of the section header for the rest.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [interaction-cost, layout, cards, imagery, hierarchy]

### G-03 Show a curated, relevant list immediately (e.g. "Top 10 easy recommended recipes")  (p.113)
- **Rule**: On open, show a curated list (the PDF's example is "Top 10 easy recommended recipes") with no tap needed. Exposing content directly has three benefits: (1) Quick value, because the user instantly sees what the app offers and is more likely to engage; (2) Reduced friction, because fewer steps means a better experience, more retention and more interaction; (3) Relevancy, because you can showcase the most relevant or popular items so the user finds something of interest right away.
- **Why**: Instant value, lower friction and higher relevance improve retention and engagement.
- **Do / Don't**: Do: a ready list of specific, useful items on first view. Don't: generic "discover" teasers (see G-02).
- **Audit check**: Does the first viewport on a content app show specific, popular or relevant items rather than teasers? Are the items curated or ranked (e.g. "Top N")?
- **Restyle fix**: Replace teaser sections with a titled curated list ("Top 10 …") of actual items, ordered by popularity or relevance.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [interaction-cost, copy, layout]

## Form fields matched to input (p.114-116)

### G-04 Design each input field for the type of data it collects  (p.114)
- **Rule**: Don't use a one-size-fits-all field. Shape each field (size, length, segmentation, typography) to match the information asked for.
- **Why**: It streamlines the interaction and removes unnecessary cognitive load, giving a smoother, more efficient experience.
- **Do / Don't**: See G-05 (verification code) and G-06 (card details) for the two examples.
- **Audit check**: Are all inputs in a form the same width and style regardless of the data they take? Is field width proportional to the expected value length?
- **Restyle fix**: Set field widths and segmentation by data type (see G-05, G-06).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [forms, interaction-cost]

### G-05 Use separate large digit boxes for verification codes  (p.114, p.115)
- **Rule**: For a verification code, use individual big boxes, one per digit (the example has four boxes), with enlarged typography for the digits, instead of one long standard-text field.
- **Why**: It minimizes the chance of error by giving each digit a designated space, makes input quicker and less error-prone, and the larger digits are easy to tell apart, which helps users who struggle with small text.
- **Do / Don't**:
  - Don't: the "Please check your e-mail" card has one wide grey single-line input with small text ("841I") and "Cancel" / "Verify" buttons.
  - Do: the same card with four separate square boxes holding large digits "8 4 1 |" (the cursor sits in the 4th box). The card has "Didn't get a code? Click to resend" centred below, and "Cancel" (text button, left) and "Verify" (filled blue, right).
- **Audit check**: Is a fixed-length code (OTP/PIN) entered into one single-line text field with default text size? Look for one `<input>` with no per-digit segmentation and font size equal to body size.
- **Restyle fix**: Replace it with N (e.g. 4) equal-sized boxes, one per digit. Use a large font size, auto-advance focus, and show a visible cursor/active state.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [forms, typography, interaction-cost]

### G-06 Match field length to expected data (short fields for CVC, expiry, zip) and pair them on a row  (p.115, p.116)
- **Rule**: Size each field by the type and amount of data it holds. A CVC is always 3 or 4 digits, so it gets a short field. Apply the same logic to expiry date (MM/YY) and zip code. Don't give every item its own full-width field.
- **Why**: Short fields remove ambiguity about what to type and make efficient use of space. The form then helps users enter data accurately and quickly, which benefits users and designers.
- **Do / Don't**:
  - Don't: the "Payment details" form is a long single column, with Card number, Expiry, CVC, Name on card, Country and Zip code each full width on its own row. The "Pay $65" button sits at the bottom, so the form is tall.
  - Do: the same form is compact. Card number is full width. Expiry and CVC sit on one row as two shorter fields (CVC has an eye icon). Name on card is full width. Country (with a flag) and Zip code sit on one row, with Zip shorter. A full-width blue "Pay $65" button sits at the bottom.
- **Audit check**: Are short-value fields (CVC 3-4 digits, expiry MM/YY, zip/postal code, quantity, age) rendered full width? Could two or more short fields share a row? A form with more than ~5 stacked full-width fields where some take ≤5 characters (inference) is a violation.
- **Restyle fix**: Set short fields to width proportional to content (about 1/3 to 1/2 of the row). Group related short fields side by side: Expiry+CVC, Country+Zip. Keep long data (card number, name) full width.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [forms, layout, spacing, interaction-cost]

## Passwords (p.117-119)

### G-07 Don't use a "Confirm password" field  (p.117, p.118)
- **Rule**: Drop the outdated "Confirm password" field. Use a single password field. Its downsides: (1) Increased interaction cost, because each extra field is another thing to do. (2) Error-prone, because a mistake in confirmation often forces users to restart the whole process, which is frustrating and slow. (3) False sense of security, because users can mistype the same way twice, defeating the purpose.
- **Why**: The extra field adds effort and frustration, and doesn't deliver the safety it promises.
- **Do / Don't**:
  - Don't: the "Set a strong password" card has "Enter password" (dots) and "Confirm password" (dots) fields and a purple "Create account" button.
  - Do: the card has only "Enter password" with an eye icon at the right end of the field, then a purple "Create account" button. The card is shorter.
- **Audit check**: Does a sign-up or reset form contain a second password field (name/label/placeholder matching confirm/repeat/retype password, or two `type=password` inputs)?
- **Restyle fix**: Delete the confirm field. Add a show/hide toggle (G-08) and inline validation (G-09) to the single field.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [forms, interaction-cost]

### G-08 Add a show/hide toggle to password fields  (p.118)
- **Rule**: Put a visibility toggle (eye icon) inside the password field so users can check what they typed without a second field. It keeps security while giving users control over visibility.
- **Why**: It replaces the confirm field and improves the experience of password fields.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (two states shown, both good): masked state shows dots with an "eye" icon on the right of the field. Revealed state shows the plain text "W$2SAmcxw" with an "eye-slash" icon. Both sit above the purple "Create account" button.
- **Audit check**: Does every password input have a toggle control (icon button, accessible label such as "Show password") inside or adjacent to the field? Is the icon state-reflective (eye vs eye-off)?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a trailing icon button inside the field that toggles `type=password` / `type=text`. Show eye when hidden and eye-slash when visible.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [forms, icons, accessibility, interaction-cost]

### G-09 Use inline, real-time password validation  (p.119)
- **Rule**: Give feedback as the user types, not after submit. Keep it subtle but clear. Options: small checkmarks, colour changes, four bars (a strength meter), or brief messages below the password field.
- **Why**: Users can fix mistakes immediately, which reduces frustration and smooths account creation or password reset.
- **Do / Don't**: Do: under the password field (with eye toggle) is a 4-segment thin bar. The first segment is filled pink/red and the other three are light grey. Below it, the caption "**Weak** password strength" has the word "Weak" in the red colour and the rest in grey. The purple "Create account" button follows. Don't: only show an error after submit (implied).
- **Audit check**: Does password creation show rules/strength live while typing? Check for a strength meter, per-rule checkmarks or message. Does it appear only after submit?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a 4-bar strength indicator plus a one-line label below the field. Colour the filled bars and the label by strength (red for weak). Optionally add per-rule checkmarks.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [forms, states, color, interaction-cost]

## Destructive confirmation dialogs (p.120-122)

### G-10 Make the destructive confirm button red (not green)  (p.120)
- **Rule**: In a confirmation pop-up for a destructive or irreversible action (delete/remove), use red for the "Delete"/"Remove" button, following convention. Don't use green, which signals a positive or safe action and confuses users.
- **Why**: Red signals a destructive or irreversible action. Green misleads the user about the severity.
- **Do / Don't**:
  - Don't: a dark-themed modal "Are you sure you want to delete your data? / This action can't be undone." with a single GREEN "Delete" pill button (no Cancel).
  - Do: the same modal with a red/pink "Delete" pill (from visual: pink-red filled), plus a "Cancel" text button (see G-11).
- **Audit check**: Is the button on a delete/remove/destroy dialog styled with a success/green or neutral primary colour instead of a danger colour (red)? Search for tokens like `btn-primary` or `bg-green-*` on destructive actions.
- **Restyle fix**: Use the danger/red token (e.g. `bg-red-*`/`--color-danger`) for the destructive confirm button. Keep green for positive actions.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: [color, buttons, states, dark-mode]

### G-11 Give users a way back: always include a Cancel option  (p.121)
- **Rule**: In destructive confirmations, include a visible Cancel option. Following conventions makes the interface more intuitive and reduces errors. A red button tells users it is an action to think twice about. Cancel acts as a safety net and reassures users they have control, reducing accidental errors.
- **Why**: A safety net that reassures users and reduces accidental errors.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (dark modal): title "Are you sure you want to delete your data?", sub-text "This action can't be undone.", a close "×" top right, a plain white-text "Cancel" on the left, and a pink/red "Delete" pill on the right. Don't: a modal with only "Delete" (see p.120 bad example).
- **Audit check**: Does a destructive confirm dialog have an explicit Cancel/dismiss button, rather than only the destructive action (a close "×" alone isn't enough)? Cancel should be styled as a secondary/text button.
- **Restyle fix**: Add a low-emphasis "Cancel" (text or ghost) button beside the red destructive button.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: [buttons, states, interaction-cost, accessibility]

### G-12 Place Cancel on the left and the destructive action on the right  (p.122)
- **Rule**: In confirmation pop-ups, put "Delete" on the right (signals moving forward, progressing the action) and "Cancel" on the left (signals a step back or retreat).
- **Why**: In left-to-right reading cultures, people scan interfaces the same way. The left Cancel is the first thing read, giving a quick exit before the more severe action on the right. This follows natural scanning patterns for a logical, efficient flow and reduces accidental deletions or cancellations.
- **Do / Don't**:
  - Don't: the pink "Delete" pill on the left and the "Cancel" text on the right.
  - Do: "Cancel" text on the left and the pink "Delete" pill on the right.
- **Audit check**: In dialogs, is the destructive/confirm button left of the cancel button (in LTR locales)? In CSS: check the DOM/flex order of the footer buttons.
- **Restyle fix**: Reorder the footer to `[Cancel] [Delete]` (secondary first, primary last on the right). Note RTL locales would mirror this (inferred from the left-to-right rationale).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [buttons, layout, alignment, interaction-cost]

## Long text on product pages (p.123-126)

### G-13 Avoid long, monolithic blocks of text  (p.123)
- **Rule**: Don't present product information as one dense paragraph. Attention is scarce. Users scan rather than read word-for-word (cited: Nielsen Norman Group research). Bite-sized information is easier to process.
- **Why**: A monolithic paragraph looks visually dull, makes reading laborious, overwhelms users and makes them bounce.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't: the UltraClean Vacuum Cleaner card has the product image on the left, and on the right the title and one very long paragraph (approx. 15+ lines) of small grey text. Only a few phrases are emphasized in darker/bold ("4.8-star customer rating", "HEPA filter", "cordless design", "conserving energy"). It is hard to scan.
- **Audit check**: Are there paragraphs longer than roughly 5-6 lines (inference) (the bad example runs about 15+ lines in a narrow column) in product/description areas? Does the description lack headings, lists or icons? Is bolding the only scan aid?
- **Restyle fix**: Break the paragraph into a short intro, a labelled list of key features, and a closing sentence (see G-14).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [typography, layout, hierarchy, copy]

### G-14 Divide content into logical sections with icons (checkmarks, stars)  (p.124)
- **Rule**: Split the same text into smaller, logically organized sections: a short intro, a "Key features:" list where each feature has a bold title, a one-line description and a tick icon, and a short closing paragraph. Use icons such as tick marks for key features and stars for reviews.
- **Why**: Icons are quicker to process than text and give an instant, clear point of focus. They break up the monotony, add a visual touch, and boost engagement.
- **Do / Don't**: Do: the same card now has the title "UltraClean Vacuum Cleaner", a star rating row (5 yellow stars, "**4.8** (3,2k reviews)"), a short intro paragraph, an underlined "Key features:" label, and four stacked features with green circular check icons, each with a dark title and a grey one-line description (Powerful Suction, HEPA Filter, Energy-Efficient, User-Friendly Controls), then a closing paragraph.
- **Audit check**: Do feature or benefit lists use plain sentences inside a paragraph instead of a list? Are ratings numeric only with no star icon? Are there headings or icons marking each point?
- **Restyle fix**: Convert features into a list with a leading icon (check) + bold title + one-line description. Add a stars + score + count row under the title (e.g. 4.8 (3,2k reviews)). Keep intro and outro short.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [icons, hierarchy, typography, layout, cards]

### G-15 Lay features out in two columns  (p.125)
- **Rule**: Arrange the feature list in a two-column grid (2×2 in the example) instead of one long stack.
- **Why**: Visually it is balanced and symmetrical, uses screen real estate efficiently and looks less cluttered and more organized. For UX, users scan and compare features side-by-side with less scrolling. It also encourages chunking (grouping information into "chunks" that are easier to remember and understand).
- **Do / Don't**: Do: the same card with the features in a 2×2 grid. The icon is a small soft-green circle with a dark check, above each feature's bold title and description (Powerful Suction | HEPA Filter / Energy-Efficient | User-Friendly Controls). Thin horizontal dividers separate the intro, features and closing paragraph. Don't (relative to p.124): one long single-column feature list.
- **Audit check**: Is a list of ≥4 (inference) short, equally structured features/benefits laid out in a single tall column with a lot of unused horizontal space? Could it be a 2-column grid?
- **Restyle fix**: Use `grid grid-cols-2 gap-*` for short feature items (icon above title above 1-2 line description). Add subtle divider lines between content groups.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: [layout, cards, spacing, hierarchy]

### G-16 Go beyond the given content: add extra content types (colour-coded icons, comparison visual)  (p.126)
- **Rule**: Don't let given product descriptions or stakeholder guidelines cap the design. Use them as a starting point and look for other angles or content types that add value to the product page. The example adds: (a) distinct tinted icons per feature (instead of identical checks), and (b) a comparison bar block: a trophy icon with "3X more powerful suction compare to other brands", and two horizontal bars. The long orange bar is "UltraClean Vacuum Cleaner" and the short pale bar is "Other brands".
- **Why**: Aiming for exceptional rather than merely good: extra content that supports the product's claims adds value.
- **Do / Don't**: Do: feature icons are individual and colour-coded in light tinted circles, and the comparison bar sits between dividers above the closing paragraph. Don't: just reproduce the supplied copy as-is.
- **Audit check**: Does the page only restate the supplied copy? Are the key selling claims (e.g. "3X more powerful") backed by a visual comparison? Are feature icons all identical or generic?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a simple bar comparison for the main quantitative claim, with a labelled bar for your product (strong, long, saturated) vs. others (short, pale). Give each feature a meaningful icon in a tinted circle.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: [craft, icons, hierarchy, imagery]

## Bonus tips, part 2: content & focus (p.127-129)

### H-01 Look beyond the brief for extra persuasive content  (p.127)
- **Rule**: Don't stop at what the brief lists. Ask whether additional benefits or features missing from the brief could tip the scales; whether customer testimonials could add social proof and trust; whether a side-by-side comparison against competitors would help. When you find such opportunities, propose them to key stakeholders even if they are out of the original scope.
- **Why**: They deepen user engagement and give the product a competitive edge. Never settle for "good enough" when "even better" is possible. Looking at user needs from different angles improves the whole UX, not just the UI.
- **Do / Don't**: Text only (no visual). Do: propose testimonials, comparison tables and extra benefits. Don't: implement only the literal brief.
- **Audit check**: Does a landing, pricing or product page that asks for a decision lack social proof (testimonials, ratings, logos) or any comparison against alternatives?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a testimonial block and/or a comparison section (us vs competitors) and surface unlisted benefits near the decision point. Flag these as proposals for stakeholders.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: [copy, hierarchy, craft]

### H-02 Be creative but keep every element relevant  (p.127)
- **Rule**: Include only information that users or visitors will find useful or informative. For every piece of content ask: "Does this directly benefit the user? Does it answer a question, solve a problem, or improve their understanding of the product or service?" If not, cut it or rephrase it so it is more useful.
- **Why**: Irrelevant details clutter the page, detract from the UX and decrease conversion.
- **Do / Don't**: Text only. Do: keep focused, relevant content. Don't: include as much information as possible.
- **Audit check**: For each block, copy line or decorative widget on the page, can you name the user question it answers? Flag blocks with no answer.
- **Restyle fix**: Remove or shorten content that doesn't serve a user need. Rewrite the kept content to be benefit-led.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [hierarchy, copy, layout]

### H-03 Remove unrelated ads and promos from task flows  (p.128)
- **Rule**: The main goal of most interfaces is task completion (sign up, purchase, find info). Any element that doesn't contribute to that goal is a distraction. Don't put unrelated promotional content, such as a "Book a ride to test our new Ferrari – only from $300 per day – Book now" ad with a red CTA button, next to a sign-up form.
- **Why**: Distractions increase the risk of errors, abandonment, or a frustrating experience.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, from visual): a split-screen sign-up card. The left half has "Create an account for free", email, username and password fields, a terms checkbox and a pale blue "Sign up" button. The right half is a dark Ferrari photo with the heading "Book a ride to test our new Ferrari", the price line and a bright red "Book now" button. The red CTA competes with the blue sign-up button.
- **Audit check**: Does a sign-up, login or checkout screen contain a second CTA, ad, upsell or promo unrelated to completing that form? Count the competing CTAs on the screen. There should be exactly one primary action.
- **Restyle fix**: Delete the promo and its CTA from the flow, or replace it with a non-interactive brand image (see H-04).
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: [interaction-cost, hierarchy, forms, buttons, layout]

### H-04 Use action-free imagery beside forms  (p.129)
- **Rule**: A graphic next to a form can help if it supports the brand, evokes the right feeling and improves the aesthetics, as long as it is action-free, with no text and no CTA. It then complements the task instead of competing with it.
- **Why**: Balances branding and usability, so it helps task completion without diverting focus.
- **Do / Don't**: Good (thumbs-up, from visual): the same split sign-up card. The right half is a full-bleed photo of a silver sports car on a track under a blue sky, with no text or button. The form sits on a white left half with the pale blue Sign up button as the only CTA. Don't: overlay copy, prices or buttons on the side image (compare H-03).
- **Audit check**: Does the decorative image next to a form contain any text, button or link? Does it relate to the brand or context?
- **Restyle fix**: Replace promotional side panels with a brand-relevant photo or illustration that has no copy or CTAs.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [imagery, forms, layout, hierarchy]

## Bonus tips: mobile (p.134-136)

### H-09 Place primary actions in the thumb zone  (p.134)
- **Rule**: Mobile devices are often used one-handed, mainly with the thumb. Place key elements (buttons, navigation links, CTAs) and the most-used features within the area the thumb reaches easily. Elements outside it are harder to reach and cause frustration or misclicks.
- **Why**: Better usability and a more user-friendly interface on mobile.
- **Do / Don't**: The thumb-zone map (from visual) on a phone: green "Easy to reach" is a blob in the lower-left/lower-middle of the screen. Orange "Stretch but okay" is the middle band and the area right of the green. Red "Hard to reach" is the top of the screen (including the top-left and the top corners) and the bottom-right corner. This map assumes a right-handed thumb (from visual).
- **Audit check**: On mobile layouts, are the primary CTA, primary navigation and most-used controls in the top third or the far corners? Is the key action reachable in the lower-middle area?
- **Restyle fix**: Move primary actions to the bottom of the screen (bottom bar, bottom-anchored CTA) and keep rarely used controls at the top.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: [mobile, interaction-cost, buttons, navigation, accessibility]

### H-10 Anchor onboarding CTAs low on the screen, within thumb reach  (p.135-136)
- **Rule**: Place the main CTA (and the secondary action) near the bottom of the screen where the thumb rests, not at the top. A CTA outside the thumb zone forces users to stretch or adjust their grip.
- **Why**: Inconvenient and frustrating, especially for users on the go or with limited mobility. A CTA placed in the thumb zone can be used quickly and easily with one hand.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, from visual): a real-estate welcome screen (house photo, headline "Your perfect home is just a search away!", subtext "Explore a wide array of properties right at your fingertips."). The outlined "Sign In" pill sits at the top-right of the screen, far from the thumb. Dark "Sign up" is at the bottom. Good (thumbs-up, from visual): the same screen with both buttons stacked at the bottom, full width and pill-shaped. The outlined "Sign In" is directly above the dark filled "Sign up" below the text. The image and text move upward.
- **Audit check**: Is any frequently used button (login, sign in, primary CTA) in the top corner of a mobile screen? Are the primary and secondary actions stacked together in the lower part?
- **Restyle fix**: Move top-corner auth buttons into a full-width stacked group at the bottom of the screen, with the primary (filled) below or at the very bottom.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [mobile, buttons, interaction-cost, layout]

## Bonus tips: states (p.136-140)

### H-11 Don't leave empty states bare  (p.136)
- **Rule**: An empty state must not be a blank screen with a bare message such as "You have no projects!". It must offer value and direction.
- **Why**: A bare empty state is a dead end. Users feel lost, frustrated, stuck and unsure what to do next, and it wastes an opportunity to guide and engage them.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, from visual): a mobile "Projects" screen (back arrow, title, settings gear) with just "You have no projects!" centered in the middle of a blank white card.
- **Audit check**: Does any list, table, dashboard or inbox render only plain text, or nothing, when it has zero items? Does it lack a CTA?
- **Restyle fix**: Replace with a designed empty state (see H-12).
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: [states, copy, interaction-cost]

### H-12 Build empty states from illustration, benefit headline, tips and a CTA  (p.136-137)
- **Rule**: A good empty state educates, engages and encourages action. It consists of: (1) a solution-oriented headline ("Start managing your projects and stay organised."), (2) an accompanying illustration to make it visually appealing and less daunting, (3) actionable tips ("Invite team members to collaborate on a project for better communication", "Set deadlines for tasks to keep everyone on track"), and (4) a direct CTA button ("Create new project").
- **Why**: It turns a potentially negative experience into a positive one, and the tips and CTA give clear next steps.
- **Do / Don't**: Good (thumbs-up, from visual): a "Projects" screen with a pink-tinted circular illustration (hourglass, calendar, clock, clipboard, lightbulb) at the top. Below it are a bold left-aligned headline, two short tips with small orange lightning-bolt icons in grey text, and a full-width pink pill "Create new project" button at the bottom of the card. Don't: show the bare text in H-11.
- **Audit check**: Does the empty state include all four parts: headline stating the benefit or next step, a visual, 1-3 tips (inference), and a primary CTA that creates the first item?
- **Restyle fix**: Add an illustration, rewrite the headline as an invitation, add 2 short tips with icons, and add a primary CTA button that performs the first action.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [states, imagery, copy, buttons, hierarchy]

### H-13 Error states must explain, offer a fix and match the app's style  (p.138)
- **Rule**: A good error state (form validation error, 404 page and so on) must: 1. clearly communicate what went wrong; 2. offer a clear, actionable solution to fix the problem; 3. maintain a consistent visual style with the rest of the application or website.
- **Why**: Error states are where journeys often go awry. A bad one leaves users frustrated and confused, not knowing what went wrong or how to fix it. A good one prevents frustration and guides users back on track.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, from visual): the Google 404 page shows "404. That's an error. The requested URL /doesnotexist was not found on this server. That's all we know." with a line-art robot illustration. It has no links, no suggestion and no search or action.
- **Audit check**: Does each error message state the cause in plain language, give a next step (retry, link, field fix), and use the app's regular typography, colours and components? A dead-end message such as "That's all we know" fails.
- **Restyle fix**: Rewrite the error copy as cause plus solution, add a primary link or button, and restyle it with the product's own components.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: [states, copy, forms, interaction-cost]

### H-14 Offer helpful links or suggestions on 404 pages  (p.139)
- **Rule**: A good 404 page doesn't only say the page can't be found. It also provides helpful links or suggestions that lead the user back on track.
- **Why**: It converts a negative experience into a positive one and shows you thought about the user's needs even in the error case. A bare "not found" leaves users lost and frustrated.
- **Do / Don't**: Good (thumbs-up, from visual): Airbnb 404 with the normal site header (logo, "Where to?" search, Become a Host, Sign Up, Log In) kept. A huge "Oops!" heading, "We can't seem to find the page you're looking for.", "Error code: 404", and "Here are some helpful links instead:" followed by a list of coral/red links (Home, Search, Help, Traveling on Airbnb, Hosting on Airbnb, Trust & Safety). A flat illustration of a sad girl with a spilled item sits on the right.
- **Audit check**: Does the 404 page show the global navigation and provide at least one link, search or suggestion list in addition to the message?
- **Restyle fix**: Keep the global header, add 4-6 relevant links or a search box, and show the error code in small text.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [states, navigation, copy, interaction-cost]

### H-15 Express brand personality in error pages  (p.140)
- **Rule**: Use the error state as a chance for creativity that reflects the brand's personality: on-brand illustrations, humorous copy or interactive elements that surprise and delight, while still guiding users back to the content they wanted.
- **Why**: It makes the error memorable and engaging and leaves a lasting impression, turning a frustrating moment into a positive interaction with the brand.
- **Do / Don't**: Good (thumbs-up, from visual): Disney 404 with the brand header (nav: Disney+, Parks & Travel, Movies, Shop, More, plus search box). A giant "404" has a Disney character (Wreck-It Ralph) in the "0". The humorous line is "You didn't break the internet, but we can't find what you are looking for." A search prompt ("What can KnowsMore help you find today?") with a search field and a mascot character. Don't: use a generic grey system error page (see H-13).
- **Audit check**: Is the error or 404 page generic (default server style) rather than using brand illustrations, voice or tone? Does it still include a path back (search or links)?
- **Restyle fix**: Add an on-brand illustration, a light-hearted line in the brand voice, and keep a search field or links to guide users back.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: [states, imagery, copy, craft]

## Bonus tips: navigation dropdowns (p.141-142)

### H-16 Add icons to navigation dropdown items  (p.141)
- **Rule**: Include small icons in top-navigation dropdown items. Icons are visual aids that quickly communicate the purpose or content of a menu item, making the dropdown more attractive and more usable.
- **Why**: Improves usability and appeal. Dropdowns help users reach different pages or features intuitively.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, from visual): a mega-menu dropdown (two columns: Interactive reports, Team dashboard, Limitless segmentation, Group analytics; Convert, Engage, Retain, Grow). Each item has a bold title and grey description, with no icons and no group headings. The list looks like a flat wall of text. Good (thumbs-up, from visual): the same items, each with a small purple line icon in a light-tinted circle to the left of title and description. The "Group analytics" item carries a green "New" badge.
- **Audit check**: Are dropdown or mega-menu items text-only, with no leading icon or visual marker? Are icon styles consistent (same stroke, same size, same tint)?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a consistent set of line icons (in a light-tinted circle container, from visual) to the left of each item title.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: [navigation, icons, craft]

### H-17 Group related dropdown items under category headings  (p.141-142)
- **Rule**: Group related items in a dropdown under distinct categories, so users quickly grasp the navigation structure and find what they need.
- **Why**: Helps users understand structure quickly.
- **Do / Don't**: Good (from visual): the dropdown has small purple column labels, "Products" and "Use cases" (p.141), and "Resources" and "Latest blog posts" (p.142). Bad: the same items as an unlabeled two-column list with no headings.
- **Audit check**: Does a dropdown with 6+ items (inference) have group labels or headings? Are the items logically grouped (by product, use case or resource type)?
- **Restyle fix**: Add small column labels in the accent colour (from visual) above each group, and arrange related items together.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [navigation, hierarchy, layout]

### H-18 Use images in dropdowns sparingly, only for highlighted items  (p.142)
- **Rule**: Images give a more comprehensive visual context than icons, but "less is often more". Don't put an image next to every item. Add images only to the items you want to highlight.
- **Why**: Images beside every item overwhelm users and clutter the interface.
- **Do / Don't**: Good (thumbs-up, from visual): a "Resources" dropdown with a left column of 4 icon items (Blog, Customer stories, Video tutorials, Documentation, each with a short grey description) and a right column on a light-grey panel titled "Latest blog posts". The panel has 3 items, each with a small thumbnail on the left, a bold title and a truncated description, plus an "All blog posts →" link in purple. Images appear only in the highlighted column. Don't: add thumbnails to every menu item.
- **Audit check**: Does every dropdown row carry a thumbnail? Are images reserved for featured content?
- **Restyle fix**: Keep icons for the main items. Put thumbnails only in a featured column or panel and add a link to see all.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: [navigation, imagery, hierarchy, icons]
