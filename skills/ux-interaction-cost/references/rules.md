## Interaction cost: definition (p.98-99)

### F-20 Minimise interaction cost (mental + physical + time effort)  (p.98)
- **Rule**: Interaction cost = the mental, physical and time effort a user must invest to reach their goal with a UI. A well-designed site/app reduces it. Ideal = zero (users find what they want immediately) but usually unfeasible because of feature breadth. Diagram: green straight arrow User → Goal (ideal) vs pink zig-zag path with many detours (high interaction cost).
- **Why**: Every unit of effort is friction between the user and their goal.
- **Do / Don't**: Do give the shortest, most direct path. Don't force detours (zig-zag).
- **Audit check**: For each key task, count clicks/keystrokes/scrolls/page loads/decisions and compare with the minimum possible.
- **Restyle fix**: Apply strategies F-24 to F-30.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: interaction-cost

### F-21 Know the three cost components: cognitive, physical, time  (p.98)
- **Rule**: (1) Cognitive effort: mental processing needed to navigate; complex/unintuitive UI → high load; simple/intuitive → low. (2) Physical effort: number of clicks or keystrokes, amount of scrolling, etc. (3) Time effort: duration to reach the goal; slow loading, complex navigation, task complexity, lack of instructions raise it.
- **Why**: Gives audit lens for classifying friction.
- **Do / Don't**: n/a (conceptual; diagram only).
- **Audit check**: Classify each friction point under cognitive / physical / time.
- **Restyle fix**: Pick fix per component (simplify, shorten path, speed up/add instructions).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost

### F-22 Minimise these measurable effort items  (p.99)
- **Rule**: Reduce the amount of: reading, scrolling, searching for useful information, understanding presented information, clicking/touching (without errors), typing, waiting for pages to load, and remembering information to complete a task. (Illustrated with a 15-icon grid of touch gestures — tap, swipe, drag, hold, multi-finger — from visual, decorative.)
- **Why**: These are the concrete sources of interaction cost.
- **Do / Don't**: n/a.
- **Audit check**: Checklist for a flow: long reading before action? long scroll to reach CTA? search needed? many taps/typing? loading waits? info that must be remembered across steps?
- **Restyle fix**: Cut copy, surface info, shorten forms, add autofill, speed up loads, keep context visible.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost

## Interaction cost: effective strategies (p.100-106)

### F-23 Cost is visible in the audit: don't expect zero, but target the biggest detours  (p.98)
- **Rule**: Zero interaction cost is the ultimate usability goal but is usually unrealistic; aim to reduce, not necessarily eliminate.
- **Why**: Feature-rich products inevitably require some interaction.
- **Do / Don't**: n/a.
- **Audit check**: Prioritise fixes on highest-frequency tasks with highest effort.
- **Restyle fix**: n/a (prioritisation guidance).
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: interaction-cost

### F-24 Keep related actions close (Fitts's Law)  (p.100)
- **Rule**: Closer and larger targets are faster to click. Position actions near the elements they relate to and give them a sufficient clickable area.
- **Why**: Fitts's Law — shorter distance and bigger target = less physical effort/time.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (Spotify, from visual): playback controls (shuffle, previous, play/pause, next, repeat + progress bar) in a persistent fixed bar at the bottom of the screen, highlighted in the screenshot, so users don't navigate to another screen/menu. Don't put the action far from its subject or behind a separate screen.
- **Audit check**: Distance between an action and the object it acts on; tiny hit areas (touch targets small); controls that require navigating away; CTA far from the related input.
- **Restyle fix**: Move controls adjacent to / persistently anchored with their target; enlarge hit areas.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, buttons, layout

### F-25 Minimise choice (Hick's Law) and curate recommendations  (p.101)
- **Rule**: Decision time rises with the number and complexity of choices. Reduce options; highlight a smaller set of recommended/popular items.
- **Why**: Hick's Law — fewer choices → faster decisions.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (Uber Eats, from visual): curated horizontal carousels "Popular near you" and "New on Uber Eats" (4 cards each visible, "See all" links) instead of a list of hundreds of restaurants; filters (sort, price range, dietary) in a left sidebar. Don't dump hundreds of items/menu entries at once.
- **Audit check**: Count of equal-weight options presented at once (nav items, cards, menu items, filters); is there a recommended/popular/curated subset?
- **Restyle fix**: Group into curated sets, show top N with "See all", progressively disclose the rest.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, hierarchy, layout

### F-26 Use working memory well: chunk information into ≈7 (±2) manageable groups  (p.101-102)
- **Rule**: People hold about seven items (give or take a couple) in working memory (George Miller). Organise information into meaningful, manageable chunks with clear headings.
- **Why**: Chunking reduces memory load.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (Airbnb listing, from visual): page split into separate boxed chunks — host summary ("Entire home hosted by… 10 guests · 4 bedrooms · 5 beds · 4 bathrooms"), highlights (Dedicated workspace, Unwind in the hot tub), description with "Show more", and "What this place offers" amenities grid with "Show all 21 amenities" — plus a sticky booking card (price £1,597/night, dates, guests, Reserve button, fee breakdown). Don't present a wall of text about the rental.
- **Audit check**: Long undivided text blocks; lists/menus/nav with >~7 ungrouped items; missing section headings.
- **Restyle fix**: Add headings, split into groups of ≲7, use "Show more"/"Show all N" to truncate long lists.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, layout, hierarchy

### F-27 Reduce distractions  (p.103)
- **Rule**: Remove attention-diverting elements — animated banners, pop-ups, unnecessary visuals — from task and reading flows. Keep only content and essential navigation.
- **Why**: Distractions pull users away from completing tasks and raise interaction cost.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (Medium article page, from visual): clean minimal layout, one narrow centred column, big bold title, author line, small action row (clap/comment/save/play/share), hero image, body text; top bar only search/Write/notifications/avatar; no pop-ups/ads. Don't add banners, pop-ups, ads.
- **Audit check**: Auto-playing/animated banners, modals on load, ads, decorative imagery competing with main content in task screens.
- **Restyle fix**: Remove or defer pop-ups/banners; strip non-essential visuals and nav on focus pages.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, layout, motion

### F-28 Promote recognition over recall  (p.104)
- **Rule**: Let users recognise actions rather than recall from memory: use common symbols, icons, phrases and a logical layout; show remembered state such as progress.
- **Why**: Reduces mental effort, frustration and time.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (Disney+, from visual): a "Continue Watching" row at the top (highlighted yellow box) with progress bars under the thumbnails, remembering the episode/timestamp, followed by rows "What to Watch Tonight", "Recommended For You", "Action and Adventure". Don't make users remember which episode/timestamp they were at.
- **Audit check**: Does the UI make users remember info from earlier screens/sessions? Are icons unlabeled/unconventional? Is last-state/progress/recent items surfaced?
- **Restyle fix**: Add "recent/continue" shortcuts, visible state/progress, conventional icons with labels, recently used items.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, navigation, icons

### F-29 Streamline tasks: remove or combine steps (autofill)  (p.105)
- **Rule**: Visualise the total number of steps in a process; remove or combine those that don't affect the outcome. Autofill address/credit-card details already entered.
- **Why**: Fewer steps lower physical interaction cost and raise efficiency.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (from visual): mobile checkout (Checkout → Add shipping address → Shipping address form → address field with typeahead suggestions e.g. "354 Oyster Point" → pick suggestion) — address autocomplete suggests full addresses; saved payment ("Pay with Visa •••• 4242") pre-filled. Don't make users retype saved data or go through avoidable steps.
- **Audit check**: Map steps in key flows (checkout, signup); count fields/screens; are there autofill (`autocomplete` attributes), address lookup, saved payment methods, combinable steps?
- **Restyle fix**: Merge/remove steps, enable autocomplete/address lookup, prefill known data.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, forms, mobile

### F-30 Reduce repetition: automate or batch repetitive tasks  (p.106)
- **Rule**: Automate repetitive tasks or make them easier (bulk actions); reduces physical and cognitive costs.
- **Why**: Repetition multiplies effort.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (Google Sheets, from visual): fill-drag a formula/value across a selected range (cells A1:A17 selected) so one action applies to many cells. Don't force one-by-one edits.
- **Audit check**: Tasks users repeat per item (per row, per product) without bulk select/apply-to-all/duplicate/templates.
- **Restyle fix**: Add multi-select + bulk actions, copy/duplicate, templates, defaults, remember previous choices.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, tables

## Interaction cost: worked example — product page (p.107-108)

### F-31 Don't hide options in dropdowns; show them (principle of visibility)  (p.107-108)
- **Rule**: Choices hidden in dropdowns or nested menus may not be known to exist, causing missed opportunities and frustration. Show all options upfront (e.g. colour swatches) so users see choices immediately and can try several before deciding.
- **Why**: Reduces mental effort and clicks. Baseline cost: dropdown colour = 2 clicks + scroll, quantity dropdown = 2 more clicks + scroll, then mouse move + click on "Add to cart" → adding two red chairs costs five clicks, one scroll and a short mouse movement. Improved: changing colour = 1 click + short mouse move.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad ("Ergobliss chair", $199 USD): "Colour" dropdown showing "Green ⌄" and "Quantity" dropdown "1 ⌄", with "Add to cart" (red pill) placed far right. Good: three visible colour options (selected lime-green with ring, plus mustard and salmon swatches) — no dropdown.
- **Audit check**: `<select>`/dropdown used for a small set of options (≈ ≤5-6 options (inference)) that could be shown inline; count clicks to configure and buy.
- **Restyle fix**: Replace dropdown with inline swatches / segmented control / radio chips.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, forms, states

### F-32 Replace text labels with visual samples (visual differentiation)  (p.108)
- **Rule**: Show actual colour swatches instead of colour names as text; colour used as a visual cue is quickly understood.
- **Why**: Faster recognition than reading text.
- **Do / Don't**: Do circular colour swatches (selected one outlined with ring). Don't list "Green" as plain text in a dropdown.
- **Audit check**: Colour/material/pattern/size-image options presented as text only.
- **Restyle fix**: Use swatch chips (with selected state ring) for colour/visual attributes.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, forms, imagery

### F-33 Use a quantity stepper (− / number / +) instead of a dropdown  (p.108)
- **Rule**: Provide a selector where users click a plus sign or type the number directly, making small changes faster and easier. (Selector shown as bordered box: "−  1  +".)
- **Why**: Fewer clicks and no scroll vs dropdown.
- **Do / Don't**: Do stepper with minus, editable number and plus. Don't quantity dropdown "1 ⌄".
- **Audit check**: Quantity implemented as `<select>`; stepper missing direct numeric entry.
- **Restyle fix**: Replace with stepper (−/+ buttons + editable input).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, forms

### F-34 Place the primary CTA next to the inputs it commits  (p.108)
- **Rule**: Move the "Add to cart" button closer to the quantity selector to reduce interaction (mouse-travel) cost.
- **Why**: Fitts's Law — shorter distance; related actions close.
- **Do / Don't**: Good: "Add to cart" pill immediately right of the quantity stepper, below the colour swatches. Bad: "Add to cart" at far right edge, separated from the dropdowns.
- **Audit check**: Distance between configuration inputs and the submit CTA; CTA far-right/detached.
- **Restyle fix**: Re-lay out so the CTA sits adjacent to (right of or directly below) the final input.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, buttons, layout

## Minimizing interaction cost: one-click ordering (p.109)

### G-01 Offer a primary "Buy now" (one-click order) button on product pages  (p.109)
- **Rule**: Reduce the steps between intent and purchase. Traditional shopping needs add to cart → checkout → enter shipping and payment details → confirm. One-Click Ordering lets users buy with a single click, skipping the checkout routine by using stored shipping and payment data. Add a primary "Buy now" button next to "Add to cart". Which of the two is the visually primary one depends on the business model and the UI's goals.
- **Why**: It sharply cuts the cognitive, physical and time effort (interaction cost) needed to place an order.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (shown): the Ergobliss chair product page ($199 USD) has a colour swatch selector (3 swatches, selected one ringed), a quantity stepper (− 1 +), and two pill buttons side by side. "Add to cart" is the outline/secondary style (pink outline, white fill). "Buy now" is the solid filled pink primary. No explicit bad example, but the implied bad case is a flow that forces cart → checkout → forms for every purchase.
- **Audit check**: On product or purchase screens, can a returning user with stored data complete the order in one click? Is there a one-step purchase CTA? Is the primary/secondary hierarchy between "Buy now" and "Add to cart" clear (filled vs outline) and a deliberate choice?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a filled primary "Buy now" button beside an outlined "Add to cart". Back it with saved address and payment. Keep one filled primary per decision area.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: [interaction-cost, buttons, hierarchy]
