# Colour: palette, backgrounds, dark mode, status colours — full rule set

## Color (p.66-71)

### E-02 Pick the colour scheme for mood and brand personality  (p.66)
- **Rule**: Colour is functional, not just decorative: it sets mood, conveys brand personality and influences behaviour; different colours evoke different emotional responses, so choose a scheme that makes users feel comfortable/engaged/excited as intended.
- **Why**: Colour plays a fundamental role in user interaction and experience.
- **Do / Don't**: Intro only – no example.
- **Audit check**: Does the palette communicate the intended brand mood/tone? Is each colour assigned a purpose (brand, action, status, neutral) rather than picked ad hoc?
- **Restyle fix**: Define a purposeful palette (primary/brand, neutrals, status colours) and assign roles before restyling.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: color, craft

### E-03 Use the primary/brand colour sparingly – reserve it for clickable elements  (p.66-67)
- **Rule**: Do not go all-out with brand/primary colours. Apply the primary colour selectively, mostly on interactive elements (buttons, links), so it works as a visual cue for where to click.
- **Why**: Overusing the primary colour overwhelms users, detracts from content, unbalances the design and makes the UI messy. "When everything is highlighted, nothing stands out"; it loses its role as a guide for interactivity.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (fintech app, pink brand): pink everywhere – account header text and icon tile, big pink card, filled pink Send/Request buttons, pink contact "Add new" circle, pink section titles/"See all" links, pink nav icons (from visual). Do: same screen, pink only on the payment card and small accents (active nav dot, dashed Add-new outline); Send/Request become neutral light-grey buttons, section titles/icons neutral grey/black (from visual) – the colour then marks the focal items only.
- **Audit check**: Count how much of the screen area/elements use the primary hue; are non-interactive things (headings, icons, labels, decorative blocks) coloured with the action colour? Rule of thumb: if primary colour appears on many non-clickable elements or on more than a few focal elements per screen (inference), flag. Can a user tell clickable from non-clickable by colour alone?
- **Restyle fix**: Move primary colour to the main CTAs/links/active states only; switch headings, secondary icons, secondary buttons to neutrals (greys/near-black); keep at most one or two highlighted focal items.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: color, hierarchy, buttons, craft

### E-04 Keep the overall palette balanced – don't use too many colours  (p.67)
- **Rule**: Beyond restraint with the primary colour, keep the whole colour scheme balanced; too many different colours is as harmful as overusing one.
- **Why**: Too many colours cause visual clutter and make it hard to know where to focus.
- **Do / Don't**: Text only. Don't: many competing hues on one screen. Do: a small, balanced scheme.
- **Audit check**: Count distinct non-neutral hues used on a screen (excluding status colours/data viz); flag if more than ~1 brand hue + 1 accent (the PDF gives no number – threshold is an inference) or if hues have no assigned role.
- **Restyle fix**: Collapse to one primary hue (+ optional accent) and neutrals; map remaining colours to neutral shades or status roles.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: color, hierarchy, craft

### E-05 Use neutral backgrounds (grey, beige, soft white), not vibrant ones  (p.67-68)
- **Rule**: Default to neutral backgrounds — shades of grey, beige, or soft whites — as a clean canvas so content and primary colours pop without competing.
- **Why**: Neutral backgrounds make it easier to focus on tasks/absorb information. Vibrant backgrounds clash with text and other UI elements, reduce readability, feel less clear and overwhelming, worsening UX even though they look "dynamic or creative" at first glance.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (portfolio screen): saturated deep-violet/indigo full-screen background with purple tab bar; coloured chart rings, text and list all sit on it and clash/compete (from visual). Do: same screen on near-white/very light grey background; chart rings and pink "See more" link pop; text easy to read; neutral backdrop minimises distraction.
- **Audit check**: Is the main page/screen background a saturated colour (high chroma, e.g. HSL saturation > ~50% (inference; PDF gives no number) – limit to light/mid lightness (about 25-75%) (inference); dark navy surfaces recommended in E-07 are fine) rather than white/grey/beige? Do charts/accents/text compete with it?
- **Restyle fix**: Replace the page/screen background with white/off-white/light grey (or beige); move brand colour to accents/CTAs; confine saturated fills to small areas.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: color, contrast, hierarchy

### E-06 Reserve coloured backgrounds for splash screens, headers and specialised sections  (p.69)
- **Rule**: Coloured backgrounds can work beautifully, but only in specific places: splash screens, headers, or specialised sections of an app/site. Know when and where to introduce colour so it adds to the experience.
- **Why**: Used in the right spot it enhances; used as the general background it detracts (see E-05).
- **Do / Don't**: Text only. Do: colour hero/header/splash/special band. Don't: colour the whole content area.
- **Audit check**: Is a saturated background used on content-heavy/working screens or full pages rather than on a header/hero/splash/feature section?
- **Restyle fix**: Limit the coloured background to a hero/header/section band; keep content areas neutral.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: color, layout

### E-07 Dark backgrounds are a valid, beneficial option  (p.69)
- **Rule**: Dark backgrounds are not just a trend; they give visually appealing contrast that makes text and elements pop, and are easier on the eyes in low light. Light text on dark reduces glare and flicker and lessens eye strain over extended use.
- **Why**: Engaging, readable UI; reduced eye strain in low-light/extended use.
- **Do / Don't**: Both examples are marked "good" (thumbs-up): same portfolio screen on a near-black/dark navy background with light text and coloured rings (soft white text, not pure) and on a light background (from visual).
- **Audit check**: If a dark theme exists, are surfaces dark neutral (not saturated) with light text and clear contrast? Does it keep the same hierarchy as light?
- **Restyle fix**: Provide a dark neutral palette (very dark navy/grey surfaces, light text, same accent colours) instead of a vibrant background.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: dark-mode, color, contrast

### E-08 Offer dark mode as a user-switchable option  (p.70)
- **Rule**: Provide a light/dark switch; dark mode is now a must-have for many users, not a nice-to-have.
- **Why**: Caters to a broader range of preferences and needs; makes the UI inclusive and versatile.
- **Do / Don't**: Text only (the p.69 image shows the same screen in both modes).
- **Audit check**: Does the product offer a dark theme / respect `prefers-color-scheme`? Are colours defined as theme tokens so both themes exist?
- **Restyle fix**: Define semantic colour tokens with light and dark values; add a theme toggle / honour system setting.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: dark-mode, accessibility, color

### E-09 Choose light vs dark to fit UX and brand image  (p.70)
- **Rule**: The light/dark choice should consider UX and align with the brand's image; whether crisp white or moody dark, it must complement the overall aesthetic and message.
- **Why**: Background is part of brand expression.
- **Do / Don't**: Text only.
- **Audit check**: Does the default theme match brand tone/audience context?
- **Restyle fix**: Select default theme (white vs moody dark) deliberately according to brand message.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: dark-mode, color, craft

### E-10 Don't rely on colour alone to convey state – add icon, label or pattern  (p.70)
- **Rule**: For errors, success, warnings, combine colour with other cues: icons, text labels, patterns. Example: red field border + exclamation-mark icon + message "Enter amount bigger than $1".
- **Why**: Bright red may not convey error/urgency to all users, especially those with colour-vision deficiencies or viewing in different lighting conditions.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't: "Sale amount $ 0.01 USD" input with only a red/coral border (no icon, no message). Do: same input with red border + red "!" circle icon inside the field (before the USD selector) + red helper text "Enter amount bigger than $1" below.
- **Audit check**: For each error/success/warning state, is there at least one non-colour indicator (icon AND/OR text message)? Check that state is distinguishable in greyscale. Flag fields that change only border/background colour.
- **Restyle fix**: Add a status icon and a short explanatory message next to the coloured state; keep colour as reinforcement.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: color, accessibility, forms, states, copy

### E-11 Use conventional colours for system statuses (red=error, green=success)  (p.71)
- **Rule**: When using colour for statuses, stick to widely accepted conventions: red for stop/error, green for go/success. Don't use yellow for errors or pink for success, even if it matches the brand.
- **Why**: Conventions come from years of user interaction and design studies; breaking them creates confusion and slows interaction because people must think twice about your custom colour coding.
- **Do / Don't**: Error – Don't: yellow/amber border, icon and message on the "Sale amount" field; Do: red/coral border, icon and message. Success – Don't: pink border and check icon on "Email olivia@untitledui.com"; Do: green border and green check icon.
- **Audit check**: Are error/danger states red-hued, success green-hued, (warning amber/yellow by convention)? Flag brand-colour or off-convention status colours (e.g. amber errors, pink/blue success).
- **Restyle fix**: Recolour statuses to red (error) / green (success) / amber (warning); keep icons + text.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: color, states, forms, accessibility

### E-12 Fit status colours to the brand via tone, not hue  (p.71)
- **Rule**: To harmonise status colours with the brand, vary the *tones* (shade/tint/saturation) of the conventional colours rather than replacing them with brand colours.
- **Why**: Convention is the one area where it's better to stick to what people know; tone tweaks keep recognisability while matching the palette.
- **Do / Don't**: Text only. Do: a brand-tuned red/green. Don't: switch the hue.
- **Audit check**: Is the status colour still clearly in the red/green hue families after brand adjustments? (check hue not shifted to brand colour)
- **Restyle fix**: Adjust lightness/saturation of the red/green tokens to match palette; keep hue family.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: color, states
