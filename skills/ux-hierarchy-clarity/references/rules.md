## Intro: UI + UX together (p.3-5)

### A-01 Don't ship a beautiful UI that doesn't help users finish the task  (p.3)
- **Rule**: A UI sets the tone within seconds; people stay or leave quickly. A polished visual layer (buttons, typography, graphics) is wasted if users struggle to complete basic tasks, get lost in navigation or can't find what they need. UI and UX are "two sides of the same coin"; you can't have one without the other.
- **Why**: Visual brilliance without usable flows goes to waste; the interface immediately sets the tone for the whole experience.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad example (thumbs-down): a flight-booking landing page ("Book your dream flight in just a few clicks!") with a gorgeous illustrated airplane hero, clean nav, but the only call to action is a single blue "Search flights" button. There is no way to enter From/To/Dates on the page, so the headline promise ("a few clicks") isn't supported by the UI (from visual).
- **Audit check**: Does the hero/landing promise a task (search, book, sign up) that the first screen lets the user start directly? Is the primary task reachable without extra navigation? Is there decorative imagery but no task-entry component?
- **Restyle fix**: Put the task's core input (e.g. search form) directly in the hero, keep the visual styling, and make the primary action live there.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, hierarchy

### A-02 Don't ship good UX with an unpolished, bare UI  (p.4)
- **Rule**: Even with rigorous research (personas, documented needs, mapped flows), neglecting UI quality yields a product that feels outdated or unpleasing and may lose to more visually appealing competitors.
- **Why**: A functional, user-centred product still "might not stand a chance against more visually appealing competition".
- **Do / Don't**: Bad example (thumbs-down): same flight page, now with the full search form (Return/One way, Economy class, 1 passenger, 2 luggages; From Vienna VIE → To London STN; Departure Friday 17 June; Return Sunday 19 June; Search) but styled raw: no imagery, flat grey page, black square-cornered outline box and black rectangular "Search" and "Sign up" buttons, plain text, no icons, no elevation, small headline (from visual).
- **Audit check**: Is the form functional but visually flat – default black/grey buttons, hard 1px outlines, no brand colour, no depth, no iconography, no imagery?
- **Restyle fix**: Keep the flow, add visual polish: brand colour primary button with rounded corners, soft card elevation for the search panel, small accent icons for fields/dropdowns, supporting imagery.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: color, depth

### A-03 Combine strong UX and strong UI so the product is intuitive and loved  (p.5)
- **Rule**: The magic happens when both are right: a product that looks good and is intuitive/user-friendly. UI principles are not only about making things pretty but about giving people a good experience.
- **Why**: Good aesthetics + good flow => intuitive, user-friendly, loved.
- **Do / Don't**: Good example (thumbs-up): same flight page with the illustrated airplane hero on the right and the search form placed as a floating, rounded, soft-shadowed white card overlapping the hero image; blue filled "Search" button (primary), small amber/orange accent icons on the passenger/luggage dropdowns, arrows between fields and radio dots; light-blue tinted top row for trip options; light-blue "Sign up" button in nav; active nav item has a dot (from visual).
- **Audit check**: Does the hero combine imagery + the task component? Is there one clear primary button, a card elevation separating the form from the background, and consistent accent colours/icons?
- **Restyle fix**: Elevate the form into a card (rounded corners, soft shadow), colour the primary CTA, add subtle accent icons to fields.
- **Kind**: principle
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, depth

## Visual hierarchy (p.7-12)

### A-05 Express importance through size, colour, weight and position  (p.7)
- **Rule**: Visual hierarchy = arranging elements to show relative importance and guide the eye, attention and actions, using size, colour, weight and position. If everything has the same size and colour, nothing stands out.
- **Why**: "You're basically shouting all the information at once, and nobody knows what to focus on."
- **Do / Don't**: Don't: shopping cart (Avocado $4.50, Lime $4.20, Orange $2.50, Total $11.20) where every button – three "Remove" buttons, "Continue shopping" and "Checkout" – is the identical dark-navy/purple filled rectangle, so all look equally important (thumbs-down).
- **Audit check**: Count filled/solid buttons with identical colour+size per decision group AND per viewport (the global header/nav is part of the first viewport, sticky or not; inference). More than one equal-weight primary-styled button in either, the same CTA (label + destination) filled twice in one viewport, or destructive row-level actions styled like primary CTAs = violation.
- **Restyle fix**: Assign one primary, one secondary, one tertiary style (see A-07/A-08) and demote repeated row actions.
- **Scope**: Score a filled CTA duplicated in one viewport here; more than one filled button inside one action group is A-07.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, buttons

### A-07 Make one primary action vibrant, secondary muted, tertiary text-only  (p.8)
- **Rule**: "Checkout" = primary: vibrant green filled button, easy to spot. "Continue shopping" = secondary: same size as Checkout but grey (light grey fill, dark text) so it demands less attention. "Remove" = least prominent: only red/pink coloured text with underline, no filled background.
- **Why**: Clear action ranking tells users what to do next.
- **Do / Don't**: Good (thumbs-up, right): green filled Checkout, light-grey filled Continue shopping (same size), small underlined pink/red "Remove" text link under each item. Rows keep price at right; unchanged layout otherwise.
- **Audit check**: Per action group: exactly one filled saturated button; secondary buttons use neutral/grey/outline; per-row destructive/repeated actions are text links (small, underlined or coloured text), not filled buttons. Zero filled buttons is also a violation when the group has a main action: a primary or only action rendered as a small text link (e.g. an onboarding "Get started" link under the copy) is easy to miss (inference). Buttons that differ only in hue (red/blue/green) at the same size and fill do not rank either: keep one saturated fill and neutralise the rest.
- **Restyle fix**: Primary = brand-coloured solid; secondary = neutral grey fill (same size is fine); tertiary/destructive-in-row = underlined text link in red. Promote a link-styled main action to the filled primary button.
- **Also cited as**: A-06 (p.8)
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: hierarchy, buttons

### A-08 Don't let imagery overpower the key details  (p.9)
- **Rule**: Don't make decorative/supporting elements disproportionately large. In the cart, oversized product photos steal the spotlight from product name, price and quantity.
- **Why**: Upsets visual balance (principle covered later) and pulls the eye from important details.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, left): cart with very large round product thumbnails (≈ 70px in a ≈ 250px-wide card, from visual) dwarfing the text; name/price/remove small.
- **Audit check**: Is the image/thumbnail height larger than ~2-3x the line-height of the primary text block next to it, or does the thumbnail visually outweigh name+price? (threshold from visual, no numeric value given.)
- **Restyle fix**: Reduce thumbnail size (≈ 40px in the good variant, from visual) so text carries the row.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, imagery

### A-09 Don't emphasise secondary information (e.g. quantity) over primary information  (p.9)
- **Rule**: Even when balanced, if less critical info (quantity "1 piece") is shown in larger font and higher contrast than product name or price, hierarchy is wrong. Quantity is useful but must not draw attention from what the product is and what it costs.
- **Why**: Attention goes to the least important data.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, right): small thumbnails, product name (Avocado/Lime/Orange) rendered light grey and small, "1 piece" rendered larger and darker than the name, price at right normal.
- **Audit check**: Compare font-size/colour of item name, price, and meta (quantity, SKU, date): is meta size >= name size or meta colour darker than name colour?
- **Restyle fix**: Name = darkest/medium weight; price = clear; meta (quantity) = smaller + lighter grey.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, typography

### A-10 Rank elements by user importance before designing  (p.10)
- **Rule**: Before touching the design, list all features/elements the UI must include and rank them by importance to the user; then use colour, size, position and contrast to highlight the key information and guide users.
- **Why**: Prevents emphasising the wrong elements ("a common mistake").
- **Do / Don't**: Process rule, no visual pair of its own.
- **Audit check**: For each screen, can you name the top 1-3 (inference) pieces of information/actions? Does the visual weight order match that ranking?
- **Restyle fix**: Make a ranked list; re-style so rank 1 gets the largest/darkest/most contrasted treatment, lower ranks recede.
- **Kind**: principle
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy

### A-11 In metric cards make the value bigger than the label  (p.10)
- **Rule**: In KPI/stat cards, the figures (e.g. $4,981.00, 591, 2,121, trend +3.2%) should be the most prominent text; the metric titles (Earnings, Sales, Views) should be small and secondary.
- **Why**: Users came for the numbers; large titles take focus away from the actual values.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, top row): dark cards with big bold titles "Earnings / Sales / Views" and tiny values ($4,981.00 with small green ↗3.2%, 591, 2121). Good (thumbs-up, bottom row): same dark cards with a small muted label above and a large bold value ("$4,981.00" with small green "↗ 3.2%", "591", "2,121" – thousands separator applied), sparkline at the bottom, small coloured icon dot top-right.
- **Audit check**: In stat/metric cards, font-size(value) > font-size(label)? Typical good ratio ≈ 2x+ (from visual). Are values formatted with thousand separators (2,121 vs 2121 in the bad version)?
- **Restyle fix**: Label: small (~12px), muted; value: large (~2x), bold, white/high-contrast; keep trend indicator small and coloured.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, typography

### A-12 Darker/stronger text for the title, lighter for supporting text  (p.11, reaffirmed p.96-97)
- **Rule**: Darker colours draw more attention and signal importance; lighter colours recede. Title should be dark black, supporting paragraph grey – not the reverse.
- **Why**: A grey title with black body undermines the title's importance.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, left card): title "Personal Injury Law" in grey, paragraph ("Attorneys in this field handle cases related to accidents, injuries, and wrongful deaths, seeking compensation for victims") in dark black. Good (right card): title in near-black, paragraph in mid-grey. Both cards have two small round icons (scales, gavel) on top.
- **Audit check**: Compare contrast ratio/lightness of heading vs body in the same block: heading contrast must be ≥ body contrast (heading colour darker than body on light themes; lighter on dark themes). The same colour for heading and paragraph is a violation; the paragraph must still reach 4.5:1.
- **Restyle fix**: Heading → text-primary (near-black); body → text-secondary (grey, still accessible).
- **Also cited as**: F-14 (p.96-97, typography checklist)
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, contrast

### A-13 Use a vibrant colour to highlight the key action  (p.11)
- **Rule**: Making a clickable element a vibrant colour (e.g. a blue button) contrasts with surrounding text and naturally guides the eye, reinforcing its importance.
- **Why**: Colour contrast with the rest of the content attracts attention.
- **Do / Don't**: Text-only guidance (no pair shown on this page).
- **Audit check**: Is the primary button/link the most saturated element in its region? Does it differ in hue/saturation from the neutral text around it?
- **Restyle fix**: Apply a saturated brand colour to the primary action only; keep other text neutral.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, color

### A-14 Differentiate information by size, weight, colour and icons instead of uniform label:value lists  (p.12)
- **Rule**: Don't present all information with equal styling (label: value rows). Use font weight, size, colour and visual cues like icons to direct attention to what matters (price, title, status, key specs, contact).
- **Why**: A uniform list gives no hierarchy, so users can't scan or spot key info.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, left): property card with house photo and then 8 identical rows: "Type: For sale", "Price: £1,273,279", "Location", "Size: 1634 sqft", "Bedrooms: 5", "Bathrooms: 3", then "Contact person: Jennifer Bloom", "Phone number" – same size, bold labels in left column + values in a second column. Good (thumbs-up, right): big bold price "£3,273,279" at top-left; "For sale" shown as a small pill/badge at top-right (soft yellow); title "Luxury 5-bed Villa" in bold below; grey address with a location pin icon; a row with three icon + value specs ("1634 sqft", "5 bed.", "3 bath." with bold numbers, light units) separated by thin dividers; a footer with a small avatar, name "Jennifer Bloom" in bold, and phone in grey. (Note price differs between versions in the PDF: £1,273,279 vs £3,273,279.)
- **Audit check**: Detail cards/pages that list ≥5 (inference) key:value pairs with identical font-size/weight/colour for every row; labels like "Price:" "Type:" repeated. Missing icons/badges for status, unformatted key metrics.
- **Restyle fix**: Promote price to the largest, bold; turn status into a badge; group specs into one icon+value row with dividers; drop labels where icon/format makes meaning obvious; reduce contact info to avatar + name + phone.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, typography

## Proximity (p.13)

### A-15 Group related items closely and separate unrelated ones (Law of Proximity)  (p.13)
- **Rule**: Elements close together are perceived as related; those far apart as separate (Gestalt Law of Proximity). Spacing between items must therefore encode grouping; equal spacing everywhere erases grouping.
- **Why**: Correct proximity significantly reduces cognitive load; equal gaps make users second-guess which label goes with which field.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, left form "Contact details"): the "Surname" label sits 28px below the "Name" field and 28px above its own "Surname" field – equal 28/28 spacing, ambiguous. Form fields: Name, Surname, Street address, City/Town, Occupation, Images (Browse).
- **Audit check**: For stacked label+input pairs, gap(label→its input) must be clearly smaller than gap(previous input→this label). Flag if the two gaps are equal or the ratio is < ~2:1 (inference) (the example is 12 vs 32 = ~2.7:1). Outside forms the ratio matters, not the values: spacing inside a group is clearly tighter than between groups (A-16 gives 12/32 for forms). Flag only equal or near-equal gaps.
- **Restyle fix**: Reduce label→input gap and increase field-group gap (see A-16).
- **Also cited as**: W3-14 (web:proximity-rule)
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: spacing, forms

### A-16 Use ~12px label-to-field and ~32px field-to-next-label spacing  (p.13)
- **Rule**: Example of correct application: "Surname" label 12px from its own field and 32px from the "Name" field above.
- **Why**: Closer = related; makes it crystal clear which label belongs to which field.
- **Do / Don't**: Good (thumbs-up, right): label–field gap 12px, gap to the previous field 32px (annotated with magenta 32 / 12 tags); fields are light-grey filled rounded inputs.
- **Audit check**: Form CSS: label margin-bottom ≈ 12px (or 8-12 (inference)), field-group margin-bottom ≈ 32px; i.e. inter-group gap ≥ ~2.5x intra-group gap (inference).
- **Restyle fix**: Set label margin-bottom 12px; set group gap (field → next label) 32px (Tailwind e.g. `mb-3` label, `space-y-8` between groups).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: spacing, forms

## Clarity (p.14-16)

### A-17 Clarity: make interaction understandable without confusion  (p.14)
- **Rule**: Clear interfaces let users understand how to interact without getting lost: straightforward navigation, intuitive layouts, easily readable text. A clear interface minimises time and mental effort. Three common mistakes undermine it: oversimplification, overwhelming complexity, misguided UX.
- **Why**: Lack of clarity makes tasks hard and elements' purposes unclear.
- **Do / Don't**: Intro; examples follow in A-18 to A-20.
- **Audit check**: Can a first-time user say what each control does and what happens next without guessing? Check the three failure modes below.
- **Restyle fix**: see A-18 to A-20.
- **Kind**: principle
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: interaction-cost, accessibility

### A-18 Don't oversimplify: keep essential labels and context  (p.14)
- **Rule**: Over-simplified designs lack the context users need to know what to do next. Keep text labels with icons/toggles.
- **Why**: Simplicity taken too far backfires; users can't tell what each control does.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, left "Smart home settings"): three tiles each with only a coloured icon (shield, lamp, arrows) and an off-state toggle, no text. Good (thumbs-up, right): same three tiles with labels "Home security", "Lights", "Air conditioning" plus toggles shown in both states (green = on for Home security and Air conditioning, grey = off for Lights) and meaningful icons (shield, bulb, fan).
- **Audit check**: Icon-only controls (toggles, tiles, nav) with no visible label/aria-label/tooltip; toggles whose on/off state isn't visually distinguishable or labelled.
- **Restyle fix**: Add a visible text label under/next to each icon; use clear on/off colours (green on, grey off); choose self-explanatory icons.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: icons, accessibility

### A-19 Don't overwhelm: limit options, buttons and information  (p.15)
- **Rule**: Interfaces packed with excessive options, buttons and info increase cognitive load and make decisions and finding things harder, even if you think you're giving more control.
- **Why**: Over-complexity is as bad as over-simplification.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, left): dark mobile "Dashboard" with orange accents: hamburger, "16 Habits", "Get Premium" badge, gauge, three outlined stat boxes (Average Self Rating, Habits Active, Habits Completion), two big orange buttons (Add More Habits / Track Your Habits), "Recommended Habits" list with many orange "+" buttons (hydrate, Exercise, Sleep…, Get Outside), a 3-column category grid (Mood, Productivity, Health) with more "+" items, total score, weekly score bar, FAB "+" and bottom nav of 4 tabs (Routines, Track Habits, Dashboard, Rate Day); tiny text, outlines everywhere, single orange used for everything. Good (right): calendar week strip (December 2023, M–S with the selected day 18 highlighted in lime), one big ring "Burned calories 652 Cal", three small rings (Steps 6540, Time 45 min, Heart 72 bpm), "Finished workouts" list with check items, icon-only bottom nav with selected state; lime accent, lots of space.
- **Audit check**: Number of distinct interactive elements per screen (e.g. > ~7-10 competing actions, multiple stacked sections each with own CTA); duplicated accent colour on everything; small text. (No numeric threshold is given in the PDF.)
- **Restyle fix**: Cut to a focused set of key metrics/actions; use progressive disclosure; group via rings/cards; reserve accent colour for the active/selected state.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, layout

### A-20 Don't mislead: make copy and control states say the same thing  (p.16)
- **Rule**: Even a balanced design fails clarity if the UX is incorrect or misleading: instructions and control states must agree.
- **Why**: Misleading UX makes users do the wrong action.
- **Do / Don't**: Bad (thumbs-down, left): list of Raspberries (checked), Mango, Pistachios (checked), Kiwi with title "Unselect items you want to remove" and green Save – the checked items would be the ones kept, which inverts the usual meaning and the checkbox semantic. Good (right): title "Select items you want to remove" with Mango and Kiwi checked, same Save button – checked = affected item, instruction matches the control behaviour.
- **Audit check**: Does the checkbox/toggle's checked state match the verb in the label/title (check = act on)? Do instructions use negated/inverse logic ("unselect to…")? Are default states pre-checked in a way that contradicts the instruction?
- **Restyle fix**: Rewrite the instruction to positive, direct form ("Select items you want to remove"); ensure checked = the thing that will happen; no default pre-checked contradiction.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: copy, forms

## Contrast (p.21-22)

### B-02 Use contrast for readability, guidance and accessibility  (p.21)
- **Rule**: Contrast is what makes different things on screen stand out from each other so the important ones are easy to see. It serves three goals: (1) Readability – text and images easy to read; (2) Guidance – directs user focus and encourages interaction with specific elements; (3) Accessibility – content visible to users with visual impairments, meeting standards like WCAG.
- **Why**: As listed above (readability, guidance, accessibility/WCAG compliance).
- **Do / Don't**: n/a (principle; the example is in B-03).
- **Audit check**: Does every text/background pair and every key interactive element (primary CTA, active state) stand out clearly from its surroundings? Run a contrast-ratio check on all text, labels, badges and icons.
- **Restyle fix**: Raise contrast on low-contrast text/controls; reserve the strongest contrast for the most important element on screen.
- **Kind**: principle
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: contrast, accessibility

### B-03 Check contrast ratios on coloured status badges / chips (use darker text on a tint instead of white-ish text on a saturated fill)  (p.21)
- **Rule**: Badge/pill text must meet accessible contrast. The PDF shows measured ratios: bad badges **2.84:1, 1.81:1, 1.92:1**; good badges **4.8:1, 4.94:1, 6.54:1**. (The PDF names WCAG but does not state the threshold; the standard WCAG AA minimum for normal text is 4.5:1 – external knowledge, consistent with all "good" ratios being ≥4.8.)
- **Why**: Low-ratio badges are hard to read and fail accessibility standards.
- **Do / Don't**: Row of three ticket cards ("Ticket 122 – Brainstorm ideas for a feature – Blocked", "Ticket 231 – Wireframes for the new UI – In progress", "Ticket 431 – Analyze user feedback – Blocked"). **Don't**: badges are saturated fills (red/pink "Blocked", orange "In progress", bright green "Blocked") with white text → ratios 2.84, 1.81, 1.92. **Do**: badges use a pale tinted background (light pink, light peach, light green) with a dark, same-hue text colour → ratios 4.8, 4.94, 6.54. (colours from visual)
- **Audit check**: Compute contrast ratio of badge/tag/chip text against its fill; flag < 4.5:1 (ratios in the PDF's bad example are 1.81–2.84). Look for white text on bright mid-tone fills (e.g. bright green #22c55e-like, orange, light red).
- **Restyle fix**: Switch to the tint pattern: light tint background (e.g. `*-100`) + dark text of the same hue (e.g. `*-700/800`), then re-measure until ≥ 4.5:1.
- **Scope**: Badge and chip text below 4.5:1 is reported here, once, not again under F-09.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: contrast, accessibility

### B-04 Create contrast with colour (complementary / analogous hues) while respecting accessibility  (p.22)
- **Rule**: Use complementary or analogous colours to differentiate elements; keep accessibility guidelines in mind so contrast is good.
- **Why**: Colour difference separates elements and guides attention.
- **Do / Don't**: Text only (no dedicated visual beyond B-03).
- **Audit check**: Are adjacent distinct elements (e.g. primary vs secondary action, active vs inactive tab) distinguishable by hue/lightness, and does each colour pair pass contrast checks?
- **Restyle fix**: Pick differentiating colours from a deliberate palette (complementary/analogous) and verify ratios.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: contrast, color

### B-07 Use a border or outline when elements are too similar in colour  (p.22)
- **Rule**: If elements are too close in colour (e.g. card on same-colour background, input on same-colour surface), add a well-placed border/outline to supply the missing contrast.
- **Why**: Border adds separation when colour difference is insufficient.
- **Do / Don't**: Text only.
- **Audit check**: Compare fill of component vs its parent background; if nearly identical and no border/shadow, flag (e.g. white input on white card without border).
- **Restyle fix**: Add a 1px subtle border (neutral tint) or outline to the element.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: borders, contrast

### B-09 Keep text over images legible for any image: add an overlay/gradient or a background blur  (p.22)
- **Rule**: For components with text over a background image, design for legibility at scale: light text on a dark photo may look fine, but it may fail when a lighter image is used. Make the component robust to any background by placing an overlay (with a layer blur that complements the image tone) or by using a background blur behind the text.
- **Why**: Content/images vary; legibility must not depend on one particular photo.
- **Do / Don't**: Three identical "Panda photography – By Aaron Burden" image cards. **Don't** (thumbs-down): title and small byline sit directly on the busy photo with no treatment – byline barely legible. **Do** (thumbs-up #1): a dark gradient overlay fades up from the bottom of the image behind the text. **Do** (thumbs-up #2): a blurred/translucent darkened panel (background blur) sits behind the text block at the bottom of the card. (from visual)
- **Audit check**: Is text positioned directly over a photo/video with no scrim, gradient, or `backdrop-filter: blur()`? Test with a light image; flag if contrast of text vs worst-case background < 4.5:1 (inference: WCAG AA threshold is not stated on p.22).
- **Restyle fix**: Add a bottom-to-top dark gradient scrim (or `backdrop-blur` + semi-transparent dark layer) behind the text; or move text to a solid panel.
- **Severity**: high ; **Tags**: contrast, imagery

## Simplicity (p.23-24)

### B-10 Reduce elements and content to their essentials  (p.23, reaffirmed p.24)
- **Rule**: Simplicity = reducing elements and content to their most essential forms; prioritise essential features and information so users can accomplish their goals straightforwardly. Every additional button, image or line of text clutters the UI: don't show information that isn't relevant to the task the user is trying to accomplish (p.24).
- **Why**: A simple design reduces cognitive effort and makes the interface easier to understand and navigate. Overcomplicated or cluttered designs overwhelm users, causing confusion and reduced engagement.
- **Do / Don't**: See B-11 (cart example).
- **Audit check**: For each screen, can the primary task be identified in a few seconds? Count competing elements/text lines per item; for each button, image and text line ask "does this help complete the task on this screen?" and flag the ones that don't.
- **Restyle fix**: Remove, collapse or defer non-essential elements (move them to a secondary screen or progressive disclosure); keep one clear primary action.
- **Also cited as**: B-12 (p.24)
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, interaction-cost

### B-11 Cart screen: keep items compact, show only task-relevant details, and let whitespace/weight/colour do the work  (p.23, p.24)
- **Rule**: On a cart screen show only what's needed to check out. Size the product image appropriately so it doesn't fight other elements; use whitespace, font weights and font colours to organise info.
- **Why**: User is there to check out, not read extra info; too much makes the screen confusing and hard to understand quickly. The simpler version lowers cognitive load.
- **Do / Don't**: Two mobile "Cart" screens (back arrow, title, trash icon; "Luxury grey suit", $699 with −/1/+ stepper; "Total (3 items): $1797"; "Proceed to checkout" black pill; bottom nav with 4 icons). **Don't**: big product photos; many dark/bold detail lines per item (Color: Grey, Size: Medium (M), Material: Polyester, Viewed: 3221 times, Sold: 221 times, "* Read return policy"); items tightly packed so only ~2 items fit; total set very large/bold. **Do**: smaller product image; only Color and Size shown in light grey small text under a bolder product name; price + quantity stepper on one line; generous vertical gaps between items so 3 items visible; total in modest size. (from visual)
- **Audit check**: In cart/checkout lists, count metadata lines per line-item (PDF good example: name + 2 attributes + price/qty). Flag extra marketing/social-proof data (views, sold counts), return-policy notes, oversized images that push content/total below the fold.
- **Restyle fix**: Delete non-task metadata (viewed/sold counts, material, return notes → link elsewhere), shrink the thumbnail, set secondary attributes in lighter grey and smaller size, add spacing between items.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, spacing

## Visual cues (p.47-48)

### C-17 Add illustrations/progress indicators to make step content faster to grasp  (p.47)
- **Rule**: Use visual cues (illustrations, step indicators) rather than text-only presentation; they do more than add flair – they help users understand the message faster, make information easier to grasp, and make the interaction more engaging and memorable.
- **Why**: Visuals make learning and interaction more engaging and memorable and improve understanding speed.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual): three "Step 1/2/3" cards ("Place Your Order", "On the Way", "Get It Delivered") with only small grey "Step N" label, title and description text on white. Do (from visual): the same cards each with a themed illustration at top (person with phone/boxes, courier on bicycle, courier handing pizza at a door) and a 3-segment progress bar under it (green segment marks the current step: 1st, 2nd or 3rd of the three) above "Step N", title and description.
- **Audit check**: Are step/process/feature sections text-only with no iconography, illustration or progress indicator? Is there a visual representation of what each step means and where the user is in the sequence?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a relevant illustration/icon per step and a segmented progress indicator highlighting the step's position.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: imagery, hierarchy

### C-18 Represent senders with coloured initial avatars in lists (inbox/messaging)  (p.48)
- **Rule**: In email inbox/messaging lists, add a visual cue per sender: a coloured circular icon (from a set of colours) with the sender's first initial in the middle.
- **Why**: A plain text list, while functional, misses opportunities to enhance usability; avatars help scanning and distinguishing senders and make the UI creative and visually appealing.
- **Do / Don't**: Don't (from visual): list of rows (name, date right-aligned, bold subject, grey preview truncated with ellipsis) with no leading visual. Do (from visual): same rows with a leading circle containing a white initial letter – S (pink, Sarah Johnson), S (green, Starbucks), J (purple, John Doe), L (orange, Lyft); text is shifted right of the avatar, dates remain right-aligned.
- **Audit check**: Do list items (people/senders/contacts) lack avatars or leading visual identifiers? Are avatar colours distinguishable among different senders?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a circular avatar (~same height as two lines of text, from visual) with the first-letter initial in white on a per-sender colour; keep the text column aligned.
- **Scope**: Fallback only: use the person's photo or the company logo when one exists (D-01).
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: imagery, hierarchy

## Sender representation in lists (continuation of previous chapter) (p.49)

### D-01 Represent senders with photos/logos, not plain text/initials  (p.49)
- **Rule**: In message/inbox lists, represent each sender with an image: a photo for people, a logo for companies. Row layout shown (from visual): avatar at left, sender name bold with date right-aligned on the same line, subject line below, one-line truncated preview (grey, smaller, ellipsis) below that; rows separated by thin, light divider lines inset from the card edges; list sits in a white rounded card with soft shadow on a very pale background.
- **Why**: Users can easily identify the sender and quickly understand the context of the message.
- **Do / Don't**: Do (thumbs-up, the only example on this page): Sarah Johnson and John Doe with round photo avatars, Starbucks and Lyft with round brand logos. (The "don't" counterpart is on the previous page, outside this range.)
- **Audit check**: Does a list of people/companies (inbox, contacts, activity feed, comments) show only text or generic/placeholder icons when a photo or logo is available?
- **Restyle fix**: Add a ~32px circular avatar (photo or logo) at the leading edge of each row; fall back to initials only when no image exists.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: imagery, cards

## Web patterns: Surfaces

### W1-80 Write a plain headline naming outcome and audience  (src: de-ai-landing-hero)
- **Rule**: Use a plain-text headline stating what the product does and a subtitle naming who it's for, instead of a gradient headline stuffed with adjectives like "Supercharge" or "seamless".
- **Why**: Adjective-heavy heroes signal nothing concrete.
- **Do / Don't**: Do name the outcome and audience. Don't fill headlines with supercharge/seamless/powerful.
- **Audit check**: grep hero copy for `supercharge|seamless|powerful|unleash` and `bg-clip-text text-transparent` gradients.
- **Restyle fix**: Rewrite headline as a concrete outcome; use solid text color.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: copy, typography

### W1-82 Make one primary hero button and a text-link secondary  (src: de-ai-landing-hero)
- **Rule**: Equal-sized buttons side by side divide attention; keep one primary button and make the other a text link with an arrow, shifting emphasis from 50/50 to about 90/10.
- **Why**: One obvious primary action sharpens conversion focus.
- **Do / Don't**: Do downgrade the secondary action. Don't put two identical buttons next to each other.
- **Audit check**: Check hero for two buttons with same variant/size.
- **Restyle fix**: Convert the secondary to a link with an arrow icon.
- **Scope**: Marketing heroes only: in task groups (cart, dialog, form) the secondary is a neutral button (A-07).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: buttons, hierarchy

### W1-83 Replace vague social proof with one specific quote  (src: de-ai-landing-hero)
- **Rule**: Replace "Trusted by 10,000+ users" over grey logos with one testimonial giving name, role and a quantified outcome, such as month-end close taking three hours instead of a week.
- **Why**: Numbers nobody can verify and unfamiliar logos aren't credible.
- **Do / Don't**: Do feature one named customer's role and quantifiable result. Don't use counts nobody can verify.
- **Audit check**: grep "Trusted by" with numbers and logo strips; check testimonials lack name/role/result.
- **Restyle fix**: Replace with a named testimonial including a metric.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: copy

### W1-84 Replace icon-circle feature rows with annotated product shots  (src: de-ai-landing-hero)
- **Rule**: Instead of three cards that each pair a circled icon with a single word (Fast, Secure, Easy), show one product screenshot with three annotations pointing at actual UI elements.
- **Why**: Pages with nothing concrete to show fall back on decoration; showing the product itself removes the generic feel.
- **Do / Don't**: Do show the real product. Don't build feature rows made of circled icons and single-word captions.
- **Audit check**: Look for 3-column grids of icon-in-circle + one-word titles ("Fast", "Secure", "Easy").
- **Restyle fix**: Substitute with a screenshot plus callouts.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: imagery, copy

## Web patterns: Navigation

### W2-75 Keep the hero to a headline, subhead, and one CTA  (src: landing-page-skeleton)
- **Rule**: In about 3 seconds the hero must convey what's offered, who it serves, and why it matters, using a headline, subhead, and single CTA; skip carousels or sliders.
- **Why**: Rotating slides conceal the one message that counts.
- **Do / Don't**: Do lead with a headline, subhead, and lone CTA. Don't put a rotating carousel in the hero.
- **Audit check**: grep hero sections for carousel/slider libraries (`swiper`, `embla`, `slick`) and count CTAs. A secondary rendered as a text link (W1-82) does not count as a second CTA; two buttons do.
- **Restyle fix**: Replace the carousel with one headline, subhead, and primary CTA.
- **Scope**: Counts filled buttons; a text-link secondary is fine (W1-82).
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: hierarchy, copy

## Web patterns: Fundamentals

### W3-17 Reserve one accent color for the single key action  (src: visual-hierarchy)
- **Rule**: Keep the interface neutral; reserve one accent for the one action you want taken. Contrast pairs: bold white headings over muted copy, filled primary beside ghost secondary.
- **Why**: Many colors turn hierarchy into noise.
- **Do / Don't**: Do: Use one accent for the primary action and ghost-style secondaries. Don't: Give each element its own color.
- **Audit check**: Count distinct accent/hue classes in a view; check for more than one filled primary-style button per view.
- **Restyle fix**: Neutralize secondary elements, make secondary buttons ghost/outline, and keep the accent fill only on the primary CTA. (On conflict the playbook wins: see E-31, avoid pure white; use off-white for headings; and E-03, links and active states may carry the primary colour as text or indicator.)
- **Scope**: The accent fill goes on one primary action per view; links and active states may carry the primary colour as text or an indicator (E-03) and don't count as a second accent.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: color, hierarchy

## Web patterns: AI-look tells

### W5-01 Report AI-look tells as a cluster, never one by one  (src: design-slop-score)
- **Rule**: One default pattern on its own says nothing about how a page was made; count the tells from W1-80..W1-84 and W5 together (except W5-04, a defect scored on its own; the hero tells W1-80..W1-84 also keep their own scored findings) and treat a page as generic only when several of them show up at once (the source's tiers: 0–1 low, 2–3 medium, 4 or more high).
- **Why**: Automated checks for these patterns misfire on a share of pages, and plenty of hand-made sites use one or two of them for good reasons.
- **Do / Don't**: Do give one grouped "AI-look tells" line that lists the tells found. Don't raise a separate finding for each tell, and don't claim the page was AI-generated.
- **Audit check**: Run `node scripts/slop-scan.mjs <url|file|dir>` or check the tells by hand; report the count and the tier. Below 3 tells, mention them as one `(general practice)` note; at 3 or more, one grouped finding at this rule's severity. Hero tells (W1-80..W1-84) are still scored on their own rules at their own severity and count toward the tier; the grouped line names them by ID instead of scoring them twice. Two equal hero CTAs (W1-82) come from `cta-scan`, not slop-scan. Drop any tell the brief, `DESIGN.md` or the project tokens chose explicitly (Accepted by design).
- **Restyle fix**: Fix the tells that cost clarity first (W1-80..W1-84, W5-04, W5-09), then replace the remaining defaults with one deliberate choice tied to the product (W5-02).
- **Scope**: Glass is judged by D-16, D-18 and E-01, never counted as a tell. Inter, one radius language and an unchanged framework theme that passes the hard thresholds are never tells (E-17, C-08).
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: hierarchy

### W5-02 Check whether the look was chosen for this product  (src: ai-design-slop-review)
- **Rule**: Ask whether anyone decided the type, palette and layout, rather than whether a model made them: if the same treatment would fit an unrelated product (say a booking tool, a CRM or a trading app) just as well, it is an accepted default.
- **Why**: Generic output comes from a missing brief, and its ingredients (Inter, cards, rounded corners, standard icon sets) are fine on their own; what is missing is a decision.
- **Do / Don't**: Do tie at least one visible decision (type, colour, imagery or layout) to the product's subject and audience. Don't call Inter, cards or rounded corners a defect by themselves (the playbook recommends Inter, E-17).
- **Audit check**: Put the hero and one product screen next to the product's domain and ask what in them could not belong to an unrelated product; if nothing, note it under W5-01. Inferred; needs a render or screenshot.
- **Restyle fix**: Needs a design decision: propose one product-specific choice (a real screenshot, domain imagery, a type pairing or an accent from the brand) instead of restyling everything.
- **Scope**: A default counts as one tell only when nothing in the brief, DESIGN.md or the tokens chose it; Inter and one radius language alone never count (E-17, C-08).
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: typography, color

### W5-11 Write copy that could only belong to this product  (src: slop-taxonomy)
- **Rule**: Generated pages reuse the same SaaS slogans ("build the future of …", "ship faster", "your AI-native …") and hedge with "may"; lines like these could describe any product and say nothing about this one.
- **Why**: Readers scan for what the product does for them, and stock phrases hide that answer.
- **Do / Don't**: Do name the user, the task and the result in plain words. Don't fill sections with filler adjectives and slogans (the hero headline is W1-80; button labels are W2-67).
- **Audit check**: grep visible copy for `seamless|effortless|supercharge|unleash|unlock|elevate|revolutioni[sz]e|game-?chang|cutting-edge|next-gen|empower|streamline|build the future|ship faster|AI-native|in today's`; flag 3 or more outside the hero.
- **Restyle fix**: Rewrite each flagged line as a concrete claim (who, what, with what result); delete it if no concrete claim exists.
- **Severity**: medium ; **Tags**: copy

### W5-14 Show a metric row only when it is the clearest form  (src: vercel-design-md)
- **Rule**: A row of repeated metric boxes (big number, small label) is a generated default; use one composed comparison when it makes the point more clearly, and keep each figure's unit, period and basis next to it.
- **Why**: Boxes added to fill a gap in the evidence read as filler, like the vague proof in W1-83.
- **Do / Don't**: Do show units, periods and the comparison a number belongs to. Don't add a stat banner to fill space or restate numbers shown elsewhere at equal weight.
- **Audit check**: Look for 3 or more sibling blocks each holding a large number and a short label (`text-3xl`+ followed by `text-sm`/`text-muted`), and check whether each figure states its unit, period and basis.
- **Restyle fix**: Keep figures that carry their unit, period and basis; merge the rest into one sentence or chart that compares them, or remove them.
- **Scope**: Where a metric row is the clearest form, keep it and apply A-11 (each figure with its unit, period and basis); merge or remove filler rows instead of restyling them.
- **Severity**: low ; **Tags**: copy, hierarchy
